\task{Measurement Error and Accuracy}

A pressure gauge with a measurement range of 0 - 10 bar has a quoted inaccuracy of 1.0\% of the full-scale reading.

\subtask{What is the maximum measurement error expected for this instrument?}
\begin{solutionblock}
    The maximum error expected in any measurement reading is 1.0\% of the full-scale reading, which is 10 bar for this particular instrument. Hence, the maximum likely error is:
    \[
    \text{Maximum error} = 1.0\% \times 10~\text{bar} = 0.1~\text{bar}
    \]
\end{solutionblock}

\subtask{ What is the likely measurement error expressed as a percentage of the output
 reading if this pressure gauge is measuring a pressure of 1 bar?}  
\begin{solutionblock}
    The maximum measurement error is a constant value related to the full-scale reading of the instrument, irrespective of the magnitude of the measured quantity.
    \begin{table}[ht]
        \centering
        \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|}
            \hline
            \textbf{Actual Pressure} & \textbf{Max Error (bar)} & \textbf{Error as \% of Reading} \\
            \hline
            10 bar (full scale) & 0.1 & 1.0\% \\
            5 bar & 0.1 & 2.0\% \\
            1 bar & 0.1 & 10.0\% \\
            0.5 bar & 0.1 & 20.0\% \\
            \hline
        \end{tabular}
        \caption{Relative measurement errors at different pressures}
    \end{table}

    \noindent
    At lower readings, the relative error becomes much larger. Therefore, if we are measuring pressures between 0 and 1 bar, we would not use an instrument with a measurement range of 0 - 10 bar.
\end{solutionblock}

\task{Measurement Precision}
The width of a room is measured 10 times by an ultrasonic sensor and the following measurements are obtained (in meters):
\[
3.521, 3.539, 3.538, 3.536, 3.525, 3.522, 3.534, 3.523, 3.527, 3.529
\]
The width of the same room is then measured by a calibrated steel tape that gives a reading of 3.520 m, 
which can be taken as the correct value for the width of the room.

\subtask{What is the measurement precision of the ultrasonic sensor?}
\begin{solutionblock}
    The mean (average) value of the 10 measurements made with the ultrasonic sensor is: 
    \[
    \bar{x} = 3.529~\text{m}
    \]
    The maximum deviation below this mean value is -0.008 m and the maximum deviation above the mean value is 0.01 m. 
    Thus, the precision of the ultrasonic rule can be expressed as max (-0.008, 0.01) = 0.01 m.
    \[
    \text{Precision} = 0.01~\text{m}
    \]
\end{solutionblock}

\subtask{ What is the maximum measurement inaccuracy of the ultrasonic sensor?}
\begin{solutionblock}
    The correct value of the room width has been measured as 3.520 by the calibrated steel rule. 
    All ultrasonic sensor measurements are above this, with the largest value being 3.539 m. 
    The maximum measurement error can be calculated as:
    \[
    \text{Maximum error} = 3.539-3.520 = 0.019~\text{m}~(19~\text{mm})
    \]

    Thus, the maximum measurement inaccuracy can be expressed as:
    \[
    \text{Inaccuracy} = 19~\text{mm}
    \]
\end{solutionblock}

\task{Linearity}

Suppose the instrument characteristic shown in figure below is that of a pressure sensor, 
in which the input units are pressure in bars from $1$ to $6~\si{bar}$, and the output units are expressed 
in volts from $1$ to $10~\si{\volt}$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{fig/linearity_graph.png}
    \label{fig:ex01_linearity}
\end{figure}

\subtask{What is the maximum nonlinearity expressed as a percentage of the full-scale deflection?}

\begin{solutionblock}
The maximum nonlinearity is the maximum deviation of any data point on the figure 
away from a straight line drawn through the data points. This deviation is indicated 
by the thick vertical line in the figure. The length of this line is 
$\SI{0.5}{\volt}$, while the full-scale deflection (FSD) is $\SI{10.0}{\volt}$. 

Thus, the maximum nonlinearity is calculated as:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Nonlinearity} = \frac{0.5}{10} \times 100 = 5\%.
\end{equation}

Therefore, the maximum nonlinearity is $\SI{5}{\percent}$ of the full-scale deflection.
\end{solutionblock}


\subtask{What is the resolution of the sensor as determined by the instrument characteristic given?}

\begin{solutionblock}
The resolution of the sensor, as determined from the graph, is the smallest change in input 
pressure that can be detected. For the given graph, the naked eye cannot reliably distinguish 
any smaller change than one small square on the graph paper, corresponding to one-tenth of a unit 
($0.1~\si{bar}$). 

Hence, the resolution of the pressure sensor is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Resolution} = \SI{0.1}{bar}.
\end{equation}
\end{solutionblock}

\task{Sensitivity}

The following resistance values of a platinum resistance thermometer were measured at a range of temperatures. 
Determine the measurement sensitivity of the instrument in ohms per degree Celsius (\si{\ohm\per\celsius}).

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Measured resistance values of a platinum resistance thermometer.}
    \begin{tabular}{cc}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Resistance (\si{\ohm})} & \textbf{Temperature (\si{\celsius})} \\
        \midrule
        307 & 200 \\
        314 & 230 \\
        321 & 260 \\
        328 & 290 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \label{tab:pt100_values}
\end{table}

\subtask{Determine the measurement sensitivity of the instrument.}

\begin{solutionblock}
From the data in \autoref{tab:pt100_values}, it can be observed that the relationship between 
resistance and temperature is approximately linear. For a temperature change of 
$\Delta T = 30~\si{\celsius}$, the corresponding change in resistance is 
$\Delta R = 7~\si{\ohm}$.

Hence, the measurement sensitivity of the instrument is:
\begin{equation}
    S = \frac{\Delta R}{\Delta T} = \frac{7}{30} = 0.233~\si{\ohm\per\celsius}.
\end{equation}

Therefore, the sensitivity of the platinum resistance thermometer is 
$\SI{0.233}{\ohm\per\celsius}$.
\end{solutionblock}

\task{Zero Drift}

This table shows the output measurements of a voltmeter under two sets of conditions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item use in an environment kept at $20~\si{\celsius}$, which is the temperature at which it was calibrated; and
    \item use in an environment at $50~\si{\celsius}$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Voltmeter readings at different ambient temperatures.}
    \begin{tabular}{ccc}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Input} & \textbf{Reading at \SI{20}{\celsius}} & \textbf{Reading at \SI{50}{\celsius}} \\
        \midrule
        1 & 10.2 & 10.5 \\
        2 & 20.3 & 20.6 \\
        3 & 30.7 & 31.0 \\
        4 & 40.8 & 41.1 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \label{tab:zero_drift}
\end{table}

\subtask{Determine the zero drift and the zero drift coefficient.}

\begin{solutionblock}
From \autoref{tab:zero_drift}, it is observed that each corresponding reading at 
$50~\si{\celsius}$ is consistently $\SI{0.3}{V}$ higher than the reading at 
$20~\si{\celsius}$. 

Hence, the \textbf{zero drift} is:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta V = 0.3~\si{V}.
\end{equation}

The \textbf{zero drift coefficient} is the drift magnitude divided by the 
temperature change that caused it:
\begin{equation}
    k = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta T} = \frac{0.3}{50 - 20} = \frac{0.3}{30} = 0.01~\si{V\per\celsius}.
\end{equation}

Therefore, the zero drift coefficient of the voltmeter is 
$\SI{0.01}{V\per\celsius}$.
\end{solutionblock}
