%% What is Automation? %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{What is Automation?}
	\textbf{Working definition for this course}\\[1mm]
	\emph{Automation is the purposeful application of mechanical, electrical, and computer technologies to reduce human involvement in task performance -- without necessarily removing humans from the loop.}\\[1mm]
	It combines sensor, actuator, control and communication technology with real-time software and data science.\\[1mm]
	\textbf{Key nuances}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Displace vs.\ replace:} automation often reallocates human effort to higher-value tasks rather than eliminating it.
		\item \textbf{Hardware and software co-design:} improvement may be purely software (e.g., CAD workflows) or mechatronic (robotic cells).
		\item \textbf{Scope:} wherever humans perform structured tasks -- production, retail checkout, offices, medical devices, energy systems, mobility.
		\item \textbf{Hierarchy:} machines contain smaller automated subsystems; modern systems are hybrid and interlinked.
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Why Now? Value, Impact, and Trends (1)}
	\small
	\textbf{What automation accomplishes today} (VDI/VDE-GMA outlook\footnotemark)
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Enables \textbf{flexible value networks}: rapid changeovers, mass customization, and supply-chain visibility.
		\item Integrates mechanical, electrical, and information technologies; links physical assets to their \textbf{digital representations}.
		\item Improves \textbf{quality, safety, and energy efficiency}; turns data into decisions.
		\item Engineers act as \textbf{system integrators} -- coordinating diverse components into a coherent whole.
	\end{itemize}

	\begin{itemize}
		\item From real-time control of single machines to \textbf{orchestration} of whole cells/lines and ecosystems.
		\item \textbf{Autonomous capabilities}: intelligent, networked products that perceive, decide, and act -- with bounded autonomy.
		\item \textbf{Sustainability and circularity}: traceability, footprint accounting, and lifecycle optimization.
		\item Clear \textbf{standards and security} and qualified human oversight -- humans remain central for supervision and ethical responsibility.
	\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Automation - example case of manufacturing systems}
  \textbf{Automation in manufacturing} can appear in:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Manufacturing support systems} - e.g., Computer aided design (CAD), computer aided manufacturing (CAM), computer-integrated manufacturing (CIM)\footnotemark, and material requirement planning (MRP).
    \item \textbf{Manufacturing systems} - the actual production and material-handling equipment.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{3mm}
  \textbf{Focus of this section:}
  Automation that directly reduces the level of human participation in manufacturing processes.\\
  \vspace{3mm}
  \textbf{Three principal types of automation:}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Fixed automation
    \item Programmable automation
    \item Flexible automation
  \end{enumerate}
  \footnotetext[2]{\textbf{Computer-integrated manufacturing (CIM)} - links both levels by connecting support systems directly with factory automation for a fully integrated enterprise.}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Fixed Automation %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Fixed Automation}
  \textbf{Definition:}  
  Equipment designed to perform a specific sequence of operations for one product or family of products.\\
  \vspace{2mm}
  \textbf{Key characteristics}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item High \textbf{production rates}, low to high product complexity.
    \item Very limited product variety; reconfiguration requires major setup time.
    \item High initial cost but low unit cost at large volumes.
    \item Typical of flow-line and assembly systems.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{3mm}
  \textbf{Examples:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Transfer lines for engine blocks.
    \item Automated bottling or packaging lines.
    \item Semiconductor assembly lines.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{3mm}
  \textbf{Technology note:}  
  Modern fixed systems may embed PLCs or computer control for limited adaptability, but mechanical configuration remains largely fixed.
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Programmable Automation %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Programmable Automation}
  \textbf{Definition:}  
  Automation that allows the operating sequence or equipment configuration to be changed through program modification.
  \vspace{2mm}\\
  \textbf{Key characteristics}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Handles \textbf{moderate product variety} and \textbf{medium production volumes}.
    \item Reprogramming enables new tasks, but setup/changeover time can be long.
    \item Combines mechanical flexibility with electronic control.
    \item Core element of almost all modern automated systems.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{3mm}
  \textbf{Enabling technologies:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CNC (Computer Numerical Control)} - programmable motion and tool paths.
    \item \textbf{Robotic technology} - reprogrammable manipulators for various tasks.
    \item \textbf{PLC (Programmable Logic Controller)} - discrete logic sequencing and interlocks.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{3mm}
  \textbf{Used in:} Machining, welding, assembly, and process industries.
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Flexible Automation %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Flexible Automation}
  \textbf{Definition:}  
  An extension of programmable automation with \textbf{no lost time for changeover} between products.\\
  \vspace{2mm}
  \textbf{Key characteristics}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Capable of producing different parts in any order with little or no manual intervention.
    \item Combines features of fixed and programmable automation.
    \item Uses integrated computer control, sensors, and communication networks to recognize and adjust to part variations automatically.
    \item Supports \textbf{soft product variety} (similar variants) at \textbf{moderate to high production rates}.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{3mm}
  \textbf{Examples:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Flexible manufacturing systems (FMS).
    \item CNC machining cells with automatic tool changers and pallet systems.
    \item Robotized assembly lines with vision feedback.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%% Technical System -- Definition and Structure %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Technical System -- Definition and Structure}
  \textbf{Definition}\\
  A \textbf{technical system} is characterized by its \textbf{inputs}, \textbf{outputs}, \textbf{function}, and \textbf{structure}. It comprises several \textbf{subsystems} arranged hierarchically and interacting with each other.\\
  \vspace{-1.5mm}
  \begin{columns}
    \begin{column}{0.45\textwidth}
      \textbf{Key points}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item Each subsystem has a specific function (mapping of input $\rightarrow$ output).
          \item Abstraction helps describe and model complex installations independent of their concrete hardware.
        \end{itemize}
      \textbf{Reading the diagram}\\
      \footnotesize
      Inputs are processed through subsystems $S_1,\ldots,S_n$ within defined system boundaries to produce outputs. Subsystems can themselves contain elements and control logic.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.45\textwidth}
      \begin{figure}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/lec01/tech_system.pdf}
        \caption{System and subsystem of a technical system (source: 
        \href{https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-662-69243-1}{Weyrich, M., 
        \textit{Industrial Automation and Information Technology}, 
        Springer Vieweg, Berlin/Heidelberg, 2024})}		
      \end{figure}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Technical Facility -- Concrete Realization %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Technical Facility -- Concrete Realization}
  \textbf{Definition: }
  A \textbf{technical facility} is a concrete collection of equipment, devices, and machinery that work together to achieve a purpose. It is a physical implementation of one or more technical systems.\\
  \vspace{1.5mm}
  \begin{columns}
    \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
      \textbf{Example (process plant excerpt)}
      \begin{itemize}
      \item Raw materials A and B; valves, piping, reactor; sensors and actuators.
      \item \textbf{Control signals} command actuators; \textbf{measurement signals} report states.
      \item Output is a new substance/product C.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
      \begin{figure}

\end{frame}

%% Technical Process -- Functional Abstraction %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Technical Process -- Functional Abstraction}
  \textbf{Definition (DIN IEC 60050-351 / VDI Glossary): }\\
  A \textbf{technical process} is the complete set of interacting operations by which \textbf{matter, energy, or information} is transformed, transported, or stored.
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
      \textbf{Why abstract?}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Real-time information processing and distributed integration require a \textbf{function-oriented} view.
        \item Focus on \textbf{subprocesses}, their interrelations, and \textbf{command/state variables}.
      \end{itemize}
      \vspace{1.5mm}
      \footnotesize
      \textbf{Example sequence}\\[1mm]
      P1: Filling \quad P2: Reaction \quad P3: Discharge\\
      Inputs: inflow A and B \quad Output: product C\\
      Measured/commanded: valve openings, flow rates, temperatures, levels.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
      \begin{figure}

  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

%% Relating the Terms in Practice %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Relating the Terms in Practice}
  \textbf{From concrete to abstract and back}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Technical system} -- abstract structure of subsystems and functions (what maps to what).
    \item \textbf{Technical facility} -- physical realization (hardware: valves, pumps, reactors, sensors).
    \item \textbf{Technical process} -- functional chain of operations (filling $\rightarrow$ reaction $\rightarrow$ discharge) with \textbf{inputs/outputs} and \textbf{state and command variables}.
  \end{enumerate}
  \vspace{2mm}
  \textbf{Signal viewpoints}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Inputs/Outputs}: material/energy/information entering or leaving the process.
    \item \emph{Command variables}: actuator setpoints (e.g., valve opening).
    \item \emph{State variables}: measured quantities (e.g., flow rate, temperature, level).
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{2mm}
  \textbf{Takeaway}\\
  Use \emph{system} to reason about structure, \emph{facility} to reason about hardware, and \emph{process} to reason about operations and signals--then connect all three with software and communication to realize automation.
\end{frame}

		\textbf{Automaton} & \emph{Self-acting artificial system whose behaviour is governed either discretely by given decision rules or continuously by defined relationships; its output variables are created from its input and state variables} (DIN IEC 60050-351).\\
		Automation system & Sensors, controller(s), actuators, communication and software that implement these decision rules.\\
		Structure & \textbf{Automaton = technical process + automation system}, monitored by the \textbf{user} (Weyrich, 2024) $\rightarrow$ next slide.\\
		Degree of automation & Proportion of automatic functions in the entire set of functions of a system or facility (DIN IEC 60050-351).\\
		Autonomous system & Automaton extended by perception, reflection, goal management and planning $\rightarrow$ ``Autonomous System'' slide.\\
		\bottomrule
	\end{tabular}\\[2mm]
	\small Automata are the \textbf{basic building blocks} of automation technology; the focus is shifting from isolated automata to the \textbf{orchestration} of cooperating subsystems.
\end{frame}

%% Degree of automation: from Taylorism to flexible automata %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Degree of Automation: From Taylorism to Flexible Automata}
	\textbf{Degree of automation (DIN IEC 60050-351):} \emph{proportion of automatic functions relative to the entire set of functions of a system or facility.}\\[2mm]
	\small
	\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
		\column{0.49\textwidth}
		\textbf{Taylorism and early industrial automation}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Production decomposed into \textbf{detailed tasks} via labour studies.
			\item Human vs.\ machine compared for \textbf{time, quality, and cost}.
			\item Example: a \textbf{welding robot} executes a specified seam -- reproducible quality, but setup/programming costs.
			\item \textbf{Taylor's ``vicious circle''}: ever more specialised subprocesses $\Rightarrow$ complex dependencies, inflexibility and high engineering effort for every change.
		\end{itemize}
		\column{0.49\textwidth}
		\textbf{Towards flexible automata}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Add \textbf{sensing} and feedback to compensate disturbances (e.g., thermal distortion during welding) -- without time-consuming reprogramming.
			\item Combine software with \textbf{standardised, modular systems}; move from isolated automata to \textbf{integrated automated systems}.
			\item Specify the \emph{context of use}, the \emph{degree of automation}, and the \emph{intervention rules} for the human (bounded autonomy, graceful override).
		\end{itemize}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Fundamental Components of Automation Systems}
  \textbf{Core concept:}  
  Automation systems acquire information from the technical process, process it, and influence the process again--closing the feedback loop.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Three essential elements to form a \textbf{closed-loop system}:
    \begin{enumerate}

      \item \textbf{Controller} -- processes measurement data and generates control commands.
      \item \textbf{Actuator} -- converts control signals into physical action on the process.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{itemize}

%% The Feedback Control Loop %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{The Feedback Control Loop}
  \textbf{Operating principle}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reference signal:} desired setpoint from operator or automation program.
    \item \textbf{Controller:} computes the control signal based on the error (difference between reference and actual output).
    \item \textbf{Actuator:} applies control effort (force, torque, motion, etc.) to the process.
    \item \textbf{Sensor:} measures actual output and feeds it back to the controller.
    \item \textbf{Feedback:} compensates for \textbf{disturbances} to maintain system stability and accuracy.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{2mm}
  \textbf{Modern trends}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Physical proximity of components no longer mandatory--virtualized and networked control is possible via industrial communication.
    \item Data-driven feedback enables adaptive and remote control architectures.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Actuators: Converting Signals into Action %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Actuators: Converting Signals into Action}
  \textbf{Definition:}  
  Actuators transform control signals into mechanical or other physical process variables.
  \vspace{1mm}
  \textbf{Example forms of actuation are:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Electromechanical:} DC, stepper, and servo motors; often coupled with gears and sensors for precise positioning.
    \item \textbf{Fluid systems:} Pneumatic and hydraulic drives--convert pressure or flow into linear or rotary motion.
    \item \textbf{Thermal or chemical:} Generate heat, flow, or chemical reactions to influence process variables.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{2mm}
  \textbf{Key developments}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Integrated electronics and control software → smart servo drives.
    \item Environmentally friendly pneumatics and efficient hydraulic systems.
    \item Specialized actuators: piezo, magnetostrictive, or shape-memory drives.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Control at the Center of Automation %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Control at the Center of Automation}
  \textbf{Terminology (used precisely in this course)}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Open-loop control:} controller output does \emph{not} use the measured process output.
    \item \textbf{Feedforward control (open-loop with disturbance model):} known/disturbance inputs are measured or estimated and compensated \emph{without} comparing to the output.
    \item \textbf{Closed-loop (feedback) control:} measured output is compared to a reference; the error drives the control action.
  \end{itemize}
  \textbf{Key idea} \\
  Real systems often \emph{combine} feedforward (anticipate known effects) with feedback (reject unknown disturbances) for improved performance.
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Feedforward Control with Disturbance Detection %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Feedforward Control with Disturbance Detection}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.65\textwidth}
      \textbf{Structure and assumptions}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Relationships between inputs, outputs, and disturbances are known at design time.
        \item Disturbances that are \textbf{detectable/measurable} can be injected into a compensator to pre-empt their effect.
        \item Unknown or non-detectable disturbances still pass to the output.
      \end{itemize}
    \column{0.35\textwidth}
      \centering
      \textbf{Pros/Cons}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item \(\checkmark\) Fast response, no sensor delay in the loop, can avoid unnecessary actuation.
        \item \(\times\) Model dependence; cannot correct modelling errors or unmeasured disturbances.
      \end{itemize}
  \end{columns}
  \begin{figure}
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\linewidth]{fig/lec01/feedforward_control.pdf}\\
    \caption{Feedforward Control with Disturbance Detection}
  \end{figure}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Closed-Loop (Feedback) Control and Practical Example %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Closed-Loop (Feedback) Control and Practical Example}
  \textbf{Closed-loop principle}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Measure output \(\rightarrow\) compare with reference \(\rightarrow\) control action reduces error.
    \item Rejects unknown disturbances; tolerates modelling errors.
  \end{itemize}
  \textbf{Example: room thermostat}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Feedback only:} keeps temperature at setpoint; sudden window opening (disturbance) causes controller to drive heater hard, wasting energy.
    \item \emph{With feedforward sensor logic:} detect rapid temperature drop due to ventilation, \textbf{temporarily disable} heating (open-loop action), then resume feedback control after window closes.
  \end{itemize}
  \textbf{Lesson:} combine \textbf{feedback} (robustness) with \textbf{feedforward} (anticipation) when disturbance pathways are known.
\end{frame}

%% What is Mechatronics? %%

\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{What is Mechatronics?}
  \textbf{Origin and sense of the term}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item “Mecha” (mechanism) + “tronics” (electronics): products integrate electronics/software \emph{intimately} with mechanisms--boundaries blur.
    \item A \textbf{mechatronic system}: a mechanical machine under electronic (incl.\ software) control.
    \item \textbf{Mechatronics (discipline)}: all subject matter needed to \emph{specify and develop} such systems--modelling/simulation of the machine, sensors/actuators, and the control system.
  \end{itemize}
  \textbf{Canonical scope (per text)}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mechanical systems}: elements, machines, precision mechanics
    \item \textbf{Electronic systems}: micro/power electronics, sensor and actuator tech
    \item \textbf{Information technology}: systems theory, automation, software engineering, AI
  \end{itemize}
  \textbf{Modern view}: mechatronics is a \emph{holistic} system-design activity, not just “add electronics to mechanics.”
\end{frame}

%% Hierarchy of safety requirements and safety concepts %%

\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Hierarchy of Safety Requirements and Safety Concepts}
	\small
	\textbf{Motivation:} a \textbf{safety argument} for a mechatronic system must cover mechanical \emph{and} electrical/electronic (E/E) subsystems; ISO 26262 cascades safety requirements over several levels of abstraction.
	\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
		\column{0.48\textwidth}
		\textbf{Safety requirements (ISO 26262)}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item \textbf{Safety goals} -- from the \emph{item definition} and HARA.
			\item \textbf{Functional safety requirements} -- \emph{preliminary architecture}.
			\item \textbf{Technical safety requirements} -- \emph{system design}.
			\item \textbf{Hardware/software safety requirements} -- HW/SW design.
		\end{itemize}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
		\textbf{Safety concepts (mechatronic extension)}
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item \textbf{Functional safety concept} (Pars 2) -- safe behaviour and responses.
			\item \textbf{Technical safety concept} (Pars 3) -- allocation to system elements.
			\item \textbf{E/E technical safety concept} (Pars 4) -- hardware, software and interfaces.
		\end{enumerate}
	\end{columns}
	\textbf{Challenge:} no generally accepted hierarchy exists yet for mechanical systems $\Rightarrow$ mechanical and E/E domains are unified in \emph{one} safety argument; the flow functional $\rightarrow$ technical $\rightarrow$ hardware gives traceability from hazard to implementation.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Introduction to HARA for Mechatronic Systems}
  \textbf{Purpose of HARA- Hazard Analysis and Risk Assessment:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Identify and evaluate potential hazards that can lead to unsafe conditions in a mechatronic system.
    \item Derive safety goals and requirements systematically from system-level behavior.
  \end{itemize}
  \textbf{Applied example: 4CAS (Four Corner Air Suspension System)}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item 4CAS raises or lowers vehicle body using air springs at each wheel.
    \item Sensors measure ride height; ECU adjusts pressure via electromechanical valves.
    \item Typical functions:
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Maintain level body at selected height.
        \item Adjust vehicle height for comfort, aerodynamics, or off-road conditions.
      \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
  \textbf{HARA Objective:}
  Link mechanical (pneumatic, structural) and E/E (electronic, software) subsystems within a unified safety argument.
\end{frame}

			\item Identifies hazards and operational situations.
			\item Evaluates hazardous events to establish \textbf{mechatronic safety goals}.
		\end{itemize}
		\textbf{Key steps}
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item \textbf{Initial system description} -- mechanical and E/E domains.
			\item \textbf{Mechatronic item definition} -- functional behaviour, interfaces, and assumptions.
			\item \textbf{Mechatronic HARA documentation} -- hazards, operational situations, and hazardous events.
		\end{enumerate}
		\column{0.46\textwidth}
		\centering
		\begin{figure}
			\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{fig/lec01/HARA.pdf}
			\caption{HARA design description (adapted from \href{https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/22612/}{R.~S.~Rivett, EngD thesis, University of York, 2018}).}
		\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Hazard Identification and Safety Goal Derivation %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Hazard Identification and Safety Goal Derivation}
  \textbf{Conceptual Flow (from the figure and text)}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Hazards arise from both \textbf{mechanical} (e.g., over-pressure, spring failure) and \textbf{E/E} (e.g., sensor fault, ECU error) domains.
    \item Each operational situation is evaluated to define corresponding \textbf{Hazardous Events}.
    \item Risk levels (severity, exposure, controllability) determine the \textbf{ASIL} (Automotive Safety Integrity Level).
    \item From hazardous events, \textbf{Mechatronic Safety Goals} are derived -- spanning mechanics, electronics, and software.
  \end{itemize}
  \textbf{Example (4CAS):}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Hazard:} Loss of air pressure in one corner → vehicle tilt → loss of control.
    \item \emph{Safety Goal:} Maintain stable body height and prevent rapid tilt under fault conditions.
  \end{itemize}
  \textbf{Outcome:}
  The HARA provides the foundational safety reasoning upon which functional, technical, and hardware safety concepts are built.
\end{frame}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Functional safety concept %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Functional Safety Concept [Pars 2]}
	\textbf{Objective:} translate the \emph{mechatronic safety goals} from the HARA into \emph{functional behaviour} that maintains a \textbf{safe state} under foreseeable faults (ISO 26262).\\[2mm]
	\textbf{In mechatronic systems}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Mechatronic system architecture} = mechanical + E/E architecture; functional safety requirements are assigned to \emph{both} mechanical and electronic elements.
		\item Mechanical aspects formerly treated as \emph{external measures} are internalised; external measures remain only for effects outside the system scope (e.g., mechanical end stops).
		\item The concept is independent of detailed E/E and mechanical designs, but defines the required \textbf{safe states} and control strategies.
	\end{itemize}
	\textbf{Example (4CAS):} if an air-pressure sensor fails, the ECU must detect the fault and switch to a safe mode -- e.g., lock the ride height until service.
\end{frame}

%% Technical safety concept %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Technical Safety Concept [Pars 3]}
	\textbf{Objective:} refine the functional safety requirements into implementable, verifiable \textbf{technical safety requirements} -- i.e., \emph{how} the safety goals are realised by E/E \emph{and} mechanical design decisions.\\[2mm]
	\textbf{Key aspects}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Extends the design to explicit \textbf{mechanical elements} (e.g., valves, springs, cylinders, mounts).
		\item Defines the \textbf{interfaces at the mechanical--E/E boundary} and maps mechanical design parameters to E/E performance limits.
		\item Allocates safety requirements to \textbf{E/E system elements} (controllers, sensors, software) and \textbf{mechanical system elements}.
		\item Ensures \textbf{traceability} from the safety goals down to specific design choices.
	\end{itemize}
	\textbf{Example (4CAS):} valve response time and ECU actuation timing must meet defined limits so that pressure adjustments remain stable under faults.
\end{frame}

%% Hardware / E/E technical safety concept %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Hardware / E/E Technical Safety Concept [Pars 4]}
	\small
	\textbf{Objective:} detail how E/E hardware and software implement -- and verify -- the technical safety requirements.\\[1mm]
	\textbf{Elements}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Hardware safety requirements:} quantitative targets such as random hardware failure rates, \textbf{diagnostic coverage} and single-point/latent-fault metrics (ISO 26262; cf.\ safe failure fraction in IEC 61508).
		\item \textbf{Hardware--software interface specification:} signals, timing, and fault reactions.
		\item \textbf{Physically realised system:} hardware + embedded executable + mechanical interface.
	\end{itemize}
	\textbf{Integration in mechatronic systems}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Hardware safety also covers electromechanical interfaces (actuators, sensors, converters) and safety-critical mechanical structures $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{co-validation} of mechanical and E/E subsystems.
	\end{itemize}
	\textbf{Example (4CAS):} the ECU diagnoses the height sensors in real time; if a sensor drifts, it uses a redundant input or holds the last safe pressure.
\end{frame}

%% Relationship Between Concepts %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Relationship Between Concepts}
  \textbf{Cascading Structure:}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Functional Safety Concept} defines “\emph{what}” must be safe.
    \item \textbf{Technical Safety Concept} defines “\emph{how}” it will be made safe.
    \item \textbf{Hardware/E/E Concept} defines “\emph{how it is implemented and verified}”.
  \end{enumerate}
  \textbf{4CAS Example Summary:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Functional:} Keep vehicle level under all driving modes.  
    \item \textbf{Technical:} Ensure consistent pneumatic pressure control, even with sensor or valve degradation.  
    \item \textbf{Hardware:} Verify electrical and mechanical subsystems meet reliability and timing specifications.
  \end{itemize}
  \textbf{Outcome:}
  These layers ensure complete traceability from hazard → goal → design → verification, across both mechanical and electronic domains.
\end{frame}

%% Reproducibility in automation systems %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Reproducibility in Automation Systems}
	\small
	\textbf{Why it matters}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Automation projects combine hardware, software and data -- reproducibility is essential for verification and scalability.
		\item Experiments, control logic and system behaviour must be \textbf{replicable across teams, sites or devices}; this enables traceability and transparency in industrial R\&D and academic work.
	\end{itemize}
	\textbf{Core principles}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Version control:} Git/GitHub or GitLab for PLC code, Node-RED flows and Python scripts.
		\item \textbf{Environment definition:} Docker or Conda environments reproduce runtime dependencies.
		\item \textbf{Documentation:} Markdown, Jupyter notebooks and READMEs for configuration and setup.
		\item \textbf{Automated testing:} simulate logic and signal behaviour to ensure consistent results.
	\end{itemize}
	\textbf{Example:} a student replicates a temperature-control experiment of another group by cloning its GitHub repository and launching the identical OpenPLC + Node-RED + InfluxDB Docker stack.
\end{frame}

%% Section summary %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Section Summary}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Automation} reallocates human effort; fixed, programmable and flexible automation differ in product variety, volume and changeover capability.
		\item An \textbf{automaton} (technical process + automation system, monitored by the user) is the basic building block; the \textbf{degree of automation} quantifies the share of automatic functions.
		\item Automation systems close the loop \textbf{sensor $\rightarrow$ controller $\rightarrow$ actuator}; feedback is the backbone, feedforward anticipates known disturbances.
		\item \textbf{Autonomous systems} add perception, reflection and planning -- within guard rails and under human supervision.
		\item \textbf{Functional safety} cascades from hazards and safety goals (HARA) via functional, technical and hardware safety concepts -- traceable across mechanical and E/E domains.
		\item \textbf{Open-source tools and reproducibility} (version control, defined environments) make automation projects verifiable and maintainable.
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
