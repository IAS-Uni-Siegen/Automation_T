\subsection{Introduction to Measurement Systems and Signal Processing}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Measurement and signal processing %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Measurement and Signal Processing}
	\small
	\textbf{Goal:} transform a physical phenomenon into a measurable and interpretable electrical/digital signal.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Measurement = objective assignment of a numerical value to a property.
		\item Sensors deliver low-level, often non-linear analogue outputs; signal processing and conditioning amplify, filter, linearise and standardise them for controllers and digital systems.
		\item Calibration ensures accuracy and removes offset, drift and disturbances.
	\end{itemize}
	\textbf{Key challenges:} very small signal levels $\rightarrow$ amplification; disturbance variables (e.g., temperature); non-linear characteristics $\rightarrow$ linearisation and filtering.\\[1mm]
	\textbf{Output types:} binary (on/off), pulse/frequency encoded, analogue continuous.
	\begin{block}{Core Idea}
		Sensors $\Rightarrow$ Signal Conditioning $\Rightarrow$ Data Conversion $\Rightarrow$ Processing $\Rightarrow$ Display/Control
	\end{block}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Elements of a Measurement System (2) %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Elements of a Measurement System}
	\textbf{Subsystems:}
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{enumerate}
				\item \textbf{Sensor} (input transducer) -- converts a physical quantity into an electrical signal; always draws some energy from the measured system (\emph{loading effect}).
				\item \textbf{Signal Conditioning} - amplifies, filters, linearizes.
				\item \textbf{Data Transmission} - carries signals to control or display unit.
				\item \textbf{Controller / Processor} - interprets and acts upon data.
				\item \textbf{Actuator} (output transducer) -- applies the control action to the system or process.
			\end{enumerate}

	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Advantages of Electrical Measurement Systems %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Advantages of Electrical Measurement Systems}
	\small
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Electric parameters vary with almost any physical quantity (temperature, strain, pressure).
		\item Signal energy can be amplified  -- minimal disturbance to measured system.
		\item Wide availability of integrated circuits for conditioning and processing.
		\item Data can be easily recorded, transmitted, and displayed electronically.
		\item Electrical signals are versatile and compatible with digital systems.
	\end{enumerate}
	\textbf{Primary sensor:} interacts with the measurand (e.g., diaphragm senses pressure).  \\ 
	\textbf{Secondary sensor:} converts the effect into an electrical signal (e.g., strain gauge).
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Example: Pressure $\rightarrow$ diaphragm deformation $\rightarrow$ resistance change $\rightarrow$ voltage signal.
		\item Full sensor assembly includes sensing element + package + leads.
		\item The raw signal typically has low amplitude and needs conditioning.
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Signal conditioning: functions %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Signal Conditioning: Functions}
	\small
	\textbf{Signal conditioning} prepares the sensor output for transmission, digitisation or display: weak analogue signals (mV range) are converted into standard ranges ($\pm 10$\,V or 4--20\,mA); ADCs need well-conditioned inputs.
	\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
		\column{0.49\textwidth}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item \textbf{Amplification:} boosts weak sensor signals to usable levels.
			\item \textbf{Filtering:} removes noise and unwanted frequency components.
			\item \textbf{Linearisation:} corrects non-linear sensor outputs.
			\item \textbf{Impedance matching:} ensures proper signal transfer between stages.
			\item \textbf{Level shifting:} adjusts the signal baseline for compatibility.
		\end{itemize}
		\column{0.49\textwidth}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item \textbf{Modulation/demodulation:} prepares signals for transmission or extraction.
			\item \textbf{Isolation:} prevents ground loops and protects against high voltages.
			\item \textbf{Calibration:} adjusts the system for accurate measurements.
			\item \textbf{Conversion:} between analogue and digital domains.
			\item \textbf{Data transmission:} over distances without degradation; final display analogue (pointer) or digital.
		\end{itemize}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Interfaces and Data Domains}
	\small
	\textbf{Measurement systems involve multiple data domains:}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Domains describe how information is represented or transmitted.
		\item Interface stages (ADC, DAC) convert between domains.
	\end{itemize}
	\textbf{Common domains:}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Analog:} signal amplitude represents information (voltage, current).
		\item \textbf{Time:} information encoded in timing (frequency, phase, pulse width).
		\item \textbf{Digital:} information represented numerically (binary codes, counts).
		\item \textbf{Non-electrical:} mechanical, thermal, magnetic, chemical and radiation domains -- prior to conversion into electrical form.
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Introduction to D.C. and A.C. Bridges %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Introduction to DC and AC Bridges}
	\small
	\textbf{Objective:} Bridge circuits are used to measure unknown resistance, capacitance, or inductance precisely by comparing with known components.
	Conversion of tiny sensor variations (strain gauge, RTD, capacitive sensor) into measurable voltages and principles of null deflection help to curtain noise and improve accuracy.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Based on the principle of \textbf{null deflection} -- when the bridge is balanced, no current flows through the detector.
		\item Used extensively for \textbf{signal conditioning} in sensors and transducers.
		\item D.C. bridges → resistive transducers (strain gauges, resistance temperature detectors (RTDs)).\\
			A.C. bridges → reactive transducers (inductive, capacitive).
	\end{itemize}
	\begin{block}{Bridge Balance Principle}
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{Z_1}{Z_2} = \frac{Z_3}{Z_4}
	\end{equation}
	At balance: $U_X = U_Y$ and detector current = 0.
	\end{block}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Introductory Example: Half-Bridge Measurement %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Introductory Example: Half-Bridge Measurement}
	\small
	\begin{columns}
	\column{0.5\textwidth}
		\textbf{Half-bridge concept:}
		\begin{equation}
		u_o = u_i \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}, \qquad [u_o, u_i\ \text{in V}]
		\end{equation}
		where:
		\begin{equation}
		R_1 = R + \Delta R, \quad R_2 = R.
		\end{equation}
		\textbf{Issues:}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Always produces non-zero offset: $u_o(u_{\text{stimulus}=0}) \neq 0$.
			\item Temperature drift affects balance.
		\end{itemize}
		\textbf{Use cases:}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Basic resistance sensing demonstration.
			\item Precursor to full Wheatstone bridge.
		\end{itemize}
	\column{0.5\textwidth}
		\begin{figure}
		\vspace{-2cm}
			\centering
			\includegraphics[scale=0.9]{fig/lec03/half_bridge.pdf}
			\caption{Half-Bridge Circuit (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
		\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Wheatstone DC Bridge %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Wheatstone DC Bridge}
	\small
	\begin{columns}
	\column{0.55\textwidth}
		\textbf{Balance condition:}
		\begin{equation}
			\frac{R_1}{R_2} = \frac{R_3}{R_4}, \qquad [R\ \Omega]
		\end{equation}
		\textbf{Output voltage (unbalanced):}
		\begin{equation}
			u_o = u_i\left(
			\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2} -
			\frac{R_4}{R_3+R_4}
			\right)
		\end{equation}
		\textbf{Characteristics:}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item High accuracy for small $\Delta R$.
			\item Sensitive to temperature unless compensated.
		\end{itemize}
	\column{0.45\textwidth}
		\begin{figure}
			\centering
			\includegraphics[scale=0.8]{fig/lec03/wheatstone_DC_bridge.pdf}
			\caption{Wheatstone Bridge Circuit (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
		\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
	\textbf{Use cases:}  
	Strain gauges, RTDs, precision resistance measurement.  
	\textbf{Advantages:} simple, accurate, high SNR.  
	\textbf{Disadvantages:} 4-resistor matching needed, temp-drift.
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Practical Wheatstone Bridge with Zero and Sensitivity Adjustments %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Practical Wheatstone Bridge with Zero and Sensitivity Adjustments}
	\small
	\begin{columns}
	\column{0.55\textwidth}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Variable resistor $R_4$ used for \textbf{zero adjustment}.
			\item $R_S$ introduces \textbf{sensitivity control} for the null detector.
		\end{itemize}
		\textbf{Output near balance:}
		\begin{equation}
			u_o \approx \frac{u_i}{4R}( \Delta R_1 - \Delta R_3 )
		\end{equation}
		\textbf{Use cases:}  
		Industrial load cells, pressure transducers, torque sensors.

\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Double Active Arm Bridge (Two-Element Bridge)}
	\small
	\begin{columns}
	\column{0.55\textwidth}
		\textbf{Purpose:} Increase sensitivity + temperature compensation.
		\begin{equation}
			u_o \approx \frac{u_i}{2R}(\Delta R)
		\end{equation}
		\textbf{Advantages:}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Doubles output sensitivity.
			\item Self-temperature-compensating.
		\end{itemize}
		\textbf{Use cases:}  
		Mechanical systems with tension/compression pairs (e.g., 4-arm strain gauge load cells).

%% Full Bridge with Four Active Sensors %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Full Bridge with Four Active Sensors}
	\small
	\textbf{Maximum sensitivity:}
	\begin{equation}
		u_o = \frac{u_i}{4} \left( \frac{\Delta R}{R} \right) \times 4
	\end{equation}
	\textbf{Advantages:}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Highest output level.
		\item Excellent common-mode rejection.
		\item Full temperature compensation.
	\end{itemize}
	\textbf{Use cases:} high-accuracy weighing systems, multi-axis force sensors.
\end{frame}

%% Maxwell Bridge (Inductance with Resistance) %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Maxwell Bridge (Inductance with Resistance)}
	\small
	\begin{columns}
	\column{0.65\textwidth}
		\textbf{Used for:} measuring inductance $L$ in series with resistance $R$.
		\begin{equation}
			Z_1 = \frac{R_1}{1 + j\omega R_1 C_1}
			\quad
			Z_4 = R_4 + j\omega L_4
		\end{equation}
		\textbf{From balance:}
		\begin{equation}
			R_4 = \frac{R_2 R_3}{R_1}, \qquad [\Omega]
		\end{equation}
		\begin{equation}
			L_4 = R_2 R_3 C_1, \qquad [\text{H}]
		\end{equation}
		\textbf{Advantages:}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Frequency-independent solution.
			\item Easy to balance physically.
		\end{itemize}
		\textbf{Disadvantages:} not suitable for high-Q coils.\\ 
		\textbf{Use cases:} medium inductors (10 mH–100 H).
	\column{0.35\textwidth}
		\begin{figure}
			\centering
			\includegraphics[scale=0.9]{fig/lec03/maxwell.pdf}
			\caption{Maxwell Bridge Circuit (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
		\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%% The Wien Frequency Bridge: Principle and Circuit %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{The Wien Frequency Bridge: Principle and Circuit}
	\small
	\begin{columns}
	\column{0.65\textwidth}
		\textbf{Purpose:}  
		The Wien frequency bridge is designed so that the bridge balances (i.e., $u_o = 0$) \textbf{only at one specific frequency} of the applied AC signal.  
		Thus, it acts as a \textbf{notch filter} or a selective frequency measurement circuit.
		\textbf{Circuit characteristics:}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Arms $Z_3$ and $Z_4$ are pure resistances, with $Z_4 = 2 Z_3$.
			\item Variable resistors $R_1$ and $R_2$ are mechanically ganged so both adjust simultaneously to the same value $R$.
			\item Capacitors in opposite arms $C_1$ and $C_2$ have identical values $C$.
		\end{itemize}
		\textbf{Impedances:}
		\begin{equation}
			Z_1 = \frac{R}{1 + j\omega C R}, \qquad
			Z_2 = R + \frac{1}{j\omega C}
		\end{equation}
		\begin{equation}
			Z_3 = R_3 \qquad\qquad
			Z_4 = 2R_3
		\end{equation}
	\column{0.35\textwidth}
		\begin{figure}
			\centering
			\includegraphics[scale=0.9]{fig/lec03/wein_bridge.pdf}
			\caption{Wien Frequency Bridge Circuit (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
		\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Wien Frequency Bridge: Balance Condition and Frequency (1) %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Wien Frequency Bridge: Balance Condition and Frequency (1)}
	\small
	\begin{columns}
	\column{0.65\textwidth}
	\textbf{Balance condition for AC bridges:}
	\begin{equation}
		Z_1 Z_4 = Z_2 Z_3
	\end{equation}
	Substitute impedances:
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{R}{1 + j\omega CR} \cdot 2R_3 
		= 
		\left(R + \frac{1}{j\omega C}\right) R_3
	\end{equation}
	After simplifying and separating real and imaginary parts, the balance occurs when:
	\begin{equation}
		\omega = \frac{1}{CR} \quad [\mathrm{rad/s}]
	\end{equation}
	\begin{equation}
		f = \frac{1}{2\pi CR} \quad [\mathrm{Hz}]
	\end{equation}
	\column{0.35\textwidth}
		\begin{figure}
			\centering
			\includegraphics[scale=0.9]{fig/lec03/wein_bridge.pdf}

	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Introduction to Operational Amplifiers}
	\small
	\textbf{Why Op-Amps?}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Widely used in analog signal conditioning, filtering, instrumentation, and measurement systems.
		\item Originally used in analog computers for mathematical operations: 
		addition, subtraction, differentiation, integration.
		\item Modern op-amps are compact ICs with high performance, low noise, and wide bandwidth.
	\end{itemize}
	\textbf{Role in Signal Conditioning}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Amplification of weak sensor signals.
		\item Conversion between signal domains (e.g., current–voltage).
		\item Implementation of filters and mathematical operations.
		\item Isolation between high/low impedance stages.
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%% Characteristics of the Ideal Operational Amplifier %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Characteristics of the Ideal Operational Amplifier}
	\small
	An ideal op-amp is defined by the following properties:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \textbf{Infinite open-loop gain}, $A_{\mathrm{VOL}} \rightarrow \infty$
		\item \textbf{Infinite input impedance}, $Z_{\mathrm{in}} \rightarrow \infty$  
		$\rightarrow$ No input current: $i_+ = i_- = 0$
		\item \textbf{Zero output impedance}, $Z_{\mathrm{out}} = 0$
		\item \textbf{Infinite bandwidth}  
		$\rightarrow$ constant gain from DC to $\infty$
		\item \textbf{Zero input offset voltage and current}
	\end{enumerate}
	\textbf{Implications:}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item No voltage difference between inputs under negative feedback.
		\item Output adjusts to maintain $U_+ = U_-$.
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Characteristics of the Ideal Operational Amplifier %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Golden Rules of Ideal Op-Amps}
	\small
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\textbf{Golden Rule 1:}  
			Under negative feedback, the op-amp forces  
			\begin{equation}
				U_+ = U_-
			\end{equation}
			\textbf{Golden Rule 2:} 
			No current flows into either input terminal. 
			\begin{equation}
				i_+ = i_- = 0
			\end{equation}
			\textbf{Consequences:}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item The inverting input can act as a \textbf{virtual ground} if the non-inverting terminal is grounded.
				\item The inverting node becomes a \textbf{summing point} for currents.
			\end{itemize}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{figure}
				\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{fig/lec03/basic_op_amp.pdf}
				\caption{Inverting and non-inverting feedback configurations (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
			\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Inverting Operational Amplifier %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Inverting Operational Amplifier}
	\small
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.55\textwidth}
			\textbf{Circuit Principle}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Input applied to the inverting terminal via resistor $R_i$.
				\item Feedback resistor $R_f$ returns output to inverting input.
				\item Non-inverting terminal grounded ($U_+=0$).
			\end{itemize}
			\textbf{Derivation}
			Using Golden Rules:
			\begin{equation}
				i_i = \frac{u_i}{R_i}, \quad i_f = \frac{-u_o}{R_f}
			\end{equation}
			Since $i_i = i_f$:
			\begin{equation}
				\frac{u_i}{R_i} = -\frac{u_o}{R_f}
			\end{equation}
			\begin{equation}
				\Rightarrow \frac{u_o}{u_i} = -\frac{R_f}{R_i}
			\end{equation}
		\column{0.45\textwidth}
			\centering
			\begin{figure}
				\hspace{-1cm}
				\includegraphics[scale=0.8]{fig/lec03/invert_op_amp.pdf}
				\caption{Inverting Operational Amplifier (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
			\end{figure}
			\textbf{Closed-loop gain:}
			\begin{equation}
				A_{V,CL} = -\frac{R_f}{R_i}
			\end{equation}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Non-Inverting Operational Amplifier %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Non-Inverting Operational Amplifier}
	\small
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\textbf{Key Feature:} Input is applied to the $+$ terminal, so output is in-phase.
			\begin{equation}
				e_B = u_i
			\end{equation}
			Using feedback current:
			\begin{equation}
				i_f = \frac{0 - u_o}{R_1 + R_f}
			\end{equation}
			From nodal condition:
			\begin{equation}
				i_f = \frac{u_i - u_o}{R_f}
			\end{equation}
			Solving the equations:
			\begin{equation}
				A_{V,CL} = \frac{u_o}{u_i} = 1 + \frac{R_f}{R_i}
			\end{equation}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\centering
			\begin{figure}
				\hspace{-1cm}
				\includegraphics[scale=0.8]{fig/lec03/non_invert_op_amp.pdf}
				\caption{Non-Inverting Operational Amplifier (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
			\end{figure}
			\textbf{Properties:}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item No inversion of signal.
				\item High input impedance.
			\end{itemize}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%% Ideal Op-Amp Differential Subtractor %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Ideal Op-Amp Differential Subtractor}
	\small
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.4\textwidth}
			\textbf{Deriving the Output Voltage:}
			\begin{equation}
				u_1 \frac{R_2}{R_T}
				= u_2 \frac{R_2}{R_T} + u_o \frac{R_1}{R_T}
			\end{equation}
			Cancel $R_T$ (=$R_1$ + $R_2$) and rearrange:
			\begin{equation}
				u_o = (u_1 - u_2)\frac{R_2}{R_1}
			\end{equation}
			\vspace{6pt}
			\textbf{Final Transfer Function:}
			\begin{equation}
				\boxed{
				u_o = (u_1 - u_2)\frac{R_2}{R_1}
				}
			\end{equation}
		\column{0.6\textwidth}
			\begin{figure}
				\includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{fig/lec03/differential.pdf}
				\caption{Op-Amp as Differential Subtractor (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
			\end{figure}
			\textbf{Output expression:}
			\begin{equation}
				u_o = (u_1 - u_2)\frac{R_2}{R_1}
			\end{equation}
			\vspace{-0.25cm}
			\textbf{Use Cases:}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Sensor differential measurement.
				\item Rejecting common-mode interference.
			\end{itemize}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%% Ideal Op-Amp Differential Subtractor %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Ideal Op-Amp Integrator}
	\small
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.4\textwidth}
			Input current through $R$:
			\begin{equation}
				I_i = \frac{u_i(t) - 0}{R} = \frac{u_i(t)}{R} \quad [\si{\ampere}]
			\end{equation}
			Feedback current through capacitor $C$ (voltage across $C$ is $u_C = -u_o(t)$):
			\begin{equation}
				I_f = C \, \frac{\mathrm{d}u_C}{\mathrm{d}t}
					= C \, \frac{\mathrm{d}(-u_o(t))}{\mathrm{d}t}
					= -C \, \frac{\mathrm{d}u_o(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}
			\end{equation}
			Equating $I_i = I_f$:
			\begin{equation}
				% \frac{u_i(t)}{R} = -C \, \frac{\mathrm{d}u_o(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}
				% \quad \rightarrow \quad
				\frac{\mathrm{d}u_o(t)}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\frac{1}{RC} u_i(t)
			\end{equation}
			\textbf{Integrator equation:}
			\begin{equation}
				u_o(t) = -\frac{1}{RC} \int u_i(t)\,\mathrm{d}t + u_o(0)
			\end{equation}
		\column{0.55\textwidth}
			\vspace{-1cm}
			\begin{figure}
				\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{fig/lec03/integrator.pdf}
				\caption{Op-Amp as Integrator (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
			\end{figure}
			For a properly reset integrator with $u_o(0)=0$:
			\begin{equation}
				\boxed{
				u_o(t) = -\dfrac{1}{RC} \displaystyle\int u_i(t)\,\mathrm{d}t
				}
			\end{equation}
			with $R$ in \si{\ohm}, $C$ in \si{\farad}, so $RC$ has units of \si{\second}.
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%% Ideal Operational Amplifier as a Differentiator (1) %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Ideal Operational Amplifier as a Differentiator (1)}
	\small
	\begin{columns}
			\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\textbf{Concept:}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item A differentiator produces an output proportional to the \textbf{time derivative} of the input voltage.
				\item Achieved by interchanging the feedback resistor and input capacitor of the integrator circuit.
				\item Uses \textbf{inverting} op-amp configuration.
			\end{itemize}
			\textbf{Derivation:}
			\begin{align*}
				i_i &= C \frac{d u_i}{dt} \\
				i_f &= \frac{-u_o}{R} \\
				i_i &= i_f \quad \\
				C \frac{d u_i}{dt} &= -\frac{u_o}{R}
			\end{align*}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{figure}
				\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{fig/lec03/differentiator.pdf}
				\caption{ Op-Amp as Differentiator (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
			\end{figure}
			\textbf{Output Voltage:}
			\begin{equation}
				\boxed{
				u_o = - C R \frac{d u_i}{dt}
				}
			\end{equation}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Ideal Operational Amplifier as a Differentiator (2)}
	\small
	\textbf{Characteristics:}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Gain factor:} $CR$ (units: \si{\second})
		\item Output leads input by \SI{90}{\degree} in phase for sinusoidal signals.
	\end{itemize}
	\textbf{Advantages:}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Ideal for edge detection and high-frequency transient measurement.
		\item Produces sharp spikes for fast changes in input.
	\end{itemize}
	\textbf{Limitations (Practical):}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Highly sensitive to high-frequency noise (since noise has large derivatives).
		\item Real circuits require a small series resistor with $C$ and RC stabilisation.
	\end{itemize}
	\textbf{Applications:}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Signal processing: differentiation of waveforms.
		\item Edge/transition detection (square $\rightarrow$ impulse).
		\item Analog computing, PID controllers (derivative term).
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%% Operational Amplifier Frequency Response %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Operational Amplifier Frequency Response}
	\small
	\textbf{Key idea:} The practical operational amplifier does \emph{not} respond instantaneously to an input signal.  
	Finite internal compensation introduces a \textbf{delay} and \textbf{frequency-dependent gain}.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item At \textbf{low frequencies (DC – few Hz)}, delay is negligible.
		\item At \textbf{higher frequencies}, finite gain-bandwidth causes:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item magnitude drop (gain roll-off),
			\item phase lag between input and output,
			\item distortion for large-signal or fast-changing inputs.
		\end{itemize}
		\item Therefore, analysis uses only \textbf{small-signal sinusoidal} inputs.
	\end{itemize}
	\textbf{Model:}  
	For many op-amps, the open-loop transfer function is:
	\begin{equation}
		A_{OL}(j f) = \frac{A_{OL}}{1 + j\frac{f}{f_c}}
	\end{equation}
	where:
	\begin{equation}
		A_{OL} \text{ (DC gain)},\quad f_c \text{ (break frequency in Hz)}
	\end{equation}
\end{frame}

%% Voltage Gain in Decibels %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Voltage Gain in Decibels}
	\small
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			For sinusoidal steady-state, the gain is expressed in \textbf{decibels (dB)} as:
			\begin{equation}
				G_{\mathrm{dB}} = 20 \log_{10}\left(\frac{u_o}{u_i}\right)
			\end{equation}
			\textbf{Interpretation:}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Negative dB → attenuation.
				\item Positive dB → amplification.
				\item Note: \emph{Power} is proportional to voltage squared → doubling power corresponds to $+3$ dB.
			\end{itemize}
			\textbf{Example table values:}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item $u_o/u_i = 1$ → $0$ dB → unity gain.
				\item $u_o/u_i = 10$ → $20$ dB.
				\item $u_o/u_i = 0.5$ → $-6$ dB → quarter power.
			\end{itemize}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{table}
				\begin{tabular}{@{}ccc@{}}
					\toprule
					$\dfrac{u_o}{u_i}$ & dB & Power output \\
					\midrule
					0.1   & $-20$ &  \\
					0.5   & $-6$  & Quarter \\
					0.707 & $-3$  & Half \\
					1     & $0$   & Unity \\
					1.41  & $3$   & Double \\
					2     & $6$   &  \\
					10    & $20$  &  \\

%% Complex Gain Representation and Phase Lag %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Complex Gain Representation and Phase Lag}
	\small
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.6\textwidth}
			Amplifier gain is a \textbf{complex quantity}:
			\begin{equation}
				A = |A| e^{j\theta}
			\end{equation}
			A sinusoidal signal can be expressed as:
			\begin{equation}
				r = x + j y, \qquad |r| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2},\qquad \theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)
			\end{equation}
			\textbf{Importance:}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Phase lag increases with frequency.
				\item Causes reduction in stability (important for feedback design).
			\end{itemize}
		\column{0.4\textwidth}
			\begin{figure}[h]
			\centering
				\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{fig/lec03/complex_representation.pdf}
				\caption{Complex number representation (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
			\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Bode plot for op-amp magnitude response %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Bode Plot for Op-Amp Magnitude Response}
	\small
	\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
		\column{0.4\textwidth}
		Open-loop magnitude response:
		\begin{equation}
			|A_{OL}(jf)| = \frac{A_{OL}}{\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{f}{f_c}\right)^2}}
		\end{equation}
		\textbf{Key points:}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item $f \ll f_c$: gain $\approx A_{OL}$.
			\item $f = f_c$ (break frequency, set by the internal compensation, $f_c = 1/(2\pi RC)$): gain drops by \SI{3}{\decibel}.
			\item $f \gg f_c$: gain falls by \textbf{20\,dB/decade}.
			\item Unity-gain frequency $f_1$: $|A_{OL}(f_1)| = 1$ (0\,dB), $f_1 \approx A_{OL} \cdot f_c$ (gain--bandwidth product) -- limits the usable closed-loop bandwidth.
		\end{itemize}
		\column{0.58\textwidth}
		\begin{figure}
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{fig/lec03/freq_mag.pdf}
			\caption{Variation of operational amplifier output with frequency: output magnitude (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
		\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Bode Plot for Op-Amp Phase Response}
	\small
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.35\textwidth}
			Phase shift for single-pole op-amp:
			\begin{equation}
				\theta(f) = -\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{f}{f_c}\right)
			\end{equation}
			\textbf{Important values:}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item At $f = 0$: $\theta = 0^\circ$
				\item At $f = f_c$: $\theta = -45^\circ$
				\item At $f \gg f_c$: $\theta \rightarrow -90^\circ$
			\end{itemize}

	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Why Frequency Response Matters in Practice %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Why Frequency Response Matters in Practice}
	\small
	\textbf{1. Stability considerations}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Large phase lags $\rightarrow$ risk of oscillations in feedback systems.
		\item Phase margin must be $>$ $45^\circ$ for robustness.
	\end{itemize}
	\textbf{2. Closed-loop bandwidth limitations}
	\begin{equation}
		\text{Closed-loop bandwidth} \approx \frac{f_1}{\text{Closed-loop gain}}
	\end{equation}
	\textbf{3. Accuracy and distortion}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item High frequencies → increased error.
		\item Important for instrumentation and fast control loops.
	\end{itemize}
	\textbf{4. Noise performance}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Noise increases at high frequency.
		\item Filtering and layout become critical.
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Linear Scaling Circuits}
	\small
	\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
		\column{0.56\textwidth}
		Linear scaling circuits produce an output $u_o$ that is a linear function of the input $u_i$:
		\begin{equation}
			u_o = f(u_i) = m\,u_i + c, \qquad \frac{\Delta u_o}{\Delta u_i} = m
		\end{equation}
		with the slope or gain $m$ (dimensionless) and the output offset $c$ at zero input (V).
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Straight-line $u_o$--$u_i$ relationship with positive or negative slope: proportional output change for a change in input.
			\item No distortion or frequency dependence $\Rightarrow$ purely resistive networks.
			\item \textbf{Applications:} voltage scaling (amplification/attenuation), impedance conversion, signal addition/subtraction, voltage-to-current and current-to-voltage conversion.
		\end{itemize}
		\column{0.42\textwidth}
		\begin{figure}
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{fig/lec03/linear_ckts.pdf}
			\caption{Linear $u_o$ vs.\ $u_i$ relationships (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
		\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%% Design Requirements %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Design Requirements}
	\small
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Circuit must behave identically over relevant frequency range $\rightarrow$ resistive components essential.
		\item Selection of resistor values:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Must avoid loading the sensor/source.
			\item Should not be extremely large (bias current → offset errors).
			\item Should not be extremely small (excess current loading).
		\end{itemize}
		\item General-purpose op-amps require minimum output load:
		\begin{equation}
			R_L \geq 2\;\text{k}\Omega \text{ (check datasheet!)}
		\end{equation}
		\item In inverting amplifiers, input resistor $R_1$ determines input impedance:
		\begin{equation}
			Z_{\text{in}} = R_1
		\end{equation}
		\item High-precision resistors (0.1\% tolerance or better) recommended for accuracy.
		\item Typical realisations: inverting/non-inverting and summing amplifiers, V--I/I--V converters and buffers (previous slides) -- the basis of filters and transducer amplifiers; non-linear op-amp circuits are left for self-study.
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Transducer Amplifiers: Overview %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Transducer Amplifiers: Overview}
	\textbf{Transducers} convert one form of energy into another. In instrumentation systems, we often use transducers that convert a physical stimulus into an electrical quantity.
	\vspace{0.2cm}
	Common resistive transducers:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Strain gauges
		\item Resistance thermometers (RTDs)
		\item Thermistors
		\item Potentiometric transducers
		\item Light-dependent resistors (LDRs)
	\end{itemize}
	\vspace{0.2cm}
	Most resistive transducers are used inside a \textbf{Wheatstone bridge}. When the transducer resistance changes by a small fraction, the bridge becomes unbalanced and generates a measurable voltage.
	\vspace{0.2cm}
	\textbf{Goal}: Amplify this small differential voltage to a useful level using an operational amplifier.
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Bridge Amplifier %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Bridge Amplifier}
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item The transducer element has resistance \(R_X = R(1+a)\), where \(a\) is a small fractional change.
				\item Bridge is biased by supply \(E\), with opposite corners labelled X and Y.
				\item The op-amp forces its input nodes to the same potential: \(U_X = U_Y = V\).
				\item Small imbalance is converted into output voltage \(u_o\).
			\end{itemize}
			At node X:
			\begin{equation}
				\frac{E - U_X}{R} = \frac{U_X - u_o}{R_f} + \frac{U_X}{R} 
			\end{equation}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{figure}
				\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{fig/lec03/bridge_amp.pdf}
				\caption{Bridge amplifier with resistive transducer (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
			\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Bridge Amplifier: Solving for Output %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Bridge Amplifier: Solving for Output}
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			At node Y:
			\begin{equation}
				\frac{E - U_Y}{R} = \frac{U_Y}{R(1+a)} + \frac{U_Y}{R_f}
			\end{equation}
			Because the op-amp forces \(U_X = U_Y = V\), the above can be combined to yield the bridge output relation:
			\begin{equation}
				u_o = \left[ \frac{V}{R} - \frac{V}{R(1+a)} \right] R_f
			\end{equation}
			From analysis of the right-hand side of the bridge:
			\begin{equation}
				V = U_Y = E \left[ \frac{R(1+a) \parallel R_f}{R + (R(1+a) \parallel R_f)} \right]
			\end{equation}
		\column{0.45\textwidth}
			This simplifies to:
			\begin{equation}
				V = \frac{E R_f (1+a)}{R(1+a) + R_f + R_f(1+a)}
			\end{equation}
			Substituting back into the earlier expression for \(u_o\) gives:
			\begin{equation}
				u_o = \frac{E R_f a}{R} \cdot \frac{1}{(1+a)\left( \frac{R + R_f}{R_f} \right) + 1}
			\end{equation}
			If \(a \ll 1\), then:
			\begin{equation}
				u_o \approx \frac{E R_f}{R} a.
			\end{equation}
			\textbf{Thus the circuit behaves linearly for small transducer changes.}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Resistance measurement %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Resistance Measurement}
	\small
	\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
		\column{0.46\textwidth}
		\textbf{Goal}: measure an unknown resistance $R_X$ in situ, without disturbing the electrical environment.
		\begin{itemize}
			\item A known reference resistor $R_s$ is fed from a reference source $E_{ref}$.
			\item The op-amp holds point X at virtual earth (0\,V), so the current $E_{ref}/R_s$ must flow through $R_X$.
		\end{itemize}
		\textbf{Key relationship:}
		\begin{equation}
			u_o = -\frac{E_{ref}}{R_s}\,R_X
		\end{equation}
		The output voltage is \textbf{directly proportional} to the unknown resistance.
		\column{0.52\textwidth}
		\begin{figure}
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{fig/lec03/res_meas.pdf}
			\caption{Resistance measurement circuit (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
		\end{figure}
		\footnotesize\textbf{Advantages:} does not disturb existing circuitry connected to $R_X$; the virtual-earth node eliminates errors due to stray capacitances.
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%% Capacitance Measurement (1) %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Capacitance Measurement (1)}
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.45\textwidth}
			\textbf{Goal}: Measure small capacitances while eliminating errors due to stray capacitance to ground.
			Concept:
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Connect unknown capacitor \(C_X\) to the inverting terminal.
				\item Op-amp maintains virtual earth at X \(\Rightarrow\) stray capacitances to ground carry negligible voltage.
				\item Known capacitor \(C_s\) is used in the feedback path.
			\end{itemize}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{figure}
				\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{fig/lec03/cap_measurement.pdf}
				\caption{Capacitance measurement circuit (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
			\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Capacitance Measurement (2) %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Capacitance Measurement (2)}
	\textbf{AC gain expression:}
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{u_o}{u_i} = \frac{j \omega C_X R_f}{1 + j \omega C_s R_f}
	\end{equation}
	Assuming:
	\begin{equation}
		\omega \gg \frac{1}{2 \pi C_s R_f}
	\end{equation}
	The output magnitude becomes approximately:
	\begin{equation}
		C_X \approx \frac{u_o/u_i}{C_s}
	\end{equation}
	\textbf{Important:} Choose \(C_s\) of the same order as \(C_X\) for accuracy.
\end{frame}

%% Air speed measurement (constant-temperature anemometer) %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Air Speed Measurement (Constant-Temperature Anemometer)}
	\footnotesize
	\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
		\column{0.52\textwidth}
		\textbf{Principle:} air flowing over a heated filament cools it and changes its resistance; the bridge imbalance is sensed and amplified.
		\begin{itemize}
			\item A platinum filament (positive temperature coefficient) is heated by the bridge current.
			\item Cooling airflow lowers the filament temperature, so $R_f$ decreases.
			\item The op-amp raises the bridge voltage $U_{out}$ until balance is restored: $R_f = R_1 R_2/R_3$.
			\item $U_{out}$ rises monotonically but \textbf{non-linearly} with the air speed $v$ (King's law: $U_{out}^2 \approx A + B\,v^{n}$, $n \approx 0.5$) $\Rightarrow$ linearisation/calibration needed.
		\end{itemize}
		\textbf{Advantages:} very fast response (the feedback keeps the filament temperature constant and compensates its thermal lag), high sensitivity, good linearity around the operating point.
		\column{0.46\textwidth}
		\begin{figure}
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{fig/lec03/air_speed_measurement_2.pdf}
			\caption{Air speed measurement using heated filament and bridge (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
		\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% What is Noise? %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{What is Noise?}
	\begin{columns}[T]
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{block}{Definition}
				Noise is any \textbf{unwanted electrical signal} (voltage or current) that becomes intermixed with the wanted signal and degrades its readability.
			\end{block}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item In measurement systems, noise may originate from the sensor, wiring, environment, or electronics.
				\item If noise amplitude is comparable to or larger than the signal, \textbf{information can be lost}.
				\item Noise is typically \textbf{random} and described statistically (RMS, power spectral density).
			\end{itemize}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\vspace{-0.5cm}
			\begin{figure}
			\includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{fig/lec03/noise.pdf}
			\caption{Signal corrupted by noise (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
			\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Internal vs External Noise %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Internal vs External Noise}
	\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
		\column{0.45\textwidth}
			\begin{block}{Internal Noise}
				\begin{itemize}
					\item Generated \textbf{within} electronic components.
					\item Present even with zero input signal.
					\item Examples: thermal noise, shot noise, 1/f noise.
				\end{itemize}
			\end{block}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{block}{External Noise}
				\begin{itemize}
					\item Picked up \textbf{from surroundings}.
					\item Couples through stray capacitance, inductance, or radiation.
					\item Examples: mains interference, EMI from machines, lightning, radio transmitters.
				\end{itemize}
			\end{block}
	\end{columns}
	\vspace{0.2cm}
	\textbf{Key idea:} external noise can often be reduced by design; internal noise (thermal, shot, flicker) remains even with perfect shielding and sets the ultimate sensitivity limit, i.e., the \textbf{minimum detectable signal}.
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Signal-to-Noise Ratio (Linear Form) %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Signal-to-Noise Ratio (Linear Form)}
	\begin{block}{Definition at a given frequency / bandwidth}
		\begin{equation}
			\mathrm{SNR} = \frac{S}{N}
		\end{equation}
		where
		\begin{itemize}
			\item $S$ = wanted signal \textbf{power} in the band of interest \;[\unit{W}],
			\item $N$ = unwanted noise \textbf{power} in the same band \;[\unit{W}].
		\end{itemize}
	\end{block}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item SNR is always defined for a \textbf{specified bandwidth} $B$.
		\item If the system bandwidth increases, noise power generally increases (often proportional to $B$).
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% SNR in Decibels (dB) %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{SNR in Decibels (dB)}
	\begin{block}{Power ratio to decibels}
		\begin{equation}
			\boxed{\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}} = 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{S}{N}\right) \unit{dB}}
		\end{equation}
	\end{block}
	\textbf{Voltage or current ratios:}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item When signal and noise are measured as \textbf{RMS voltages} across the same resistance $R$:
		\begin{equation}
			\frac{S}{N} = \left(\frac{U_{S,\mathrm{rms}}}{U_{N,\mathrm{rms}}}\right)^2
			\quad \Rightarrow \quad
			\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}} = 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{U_{S,\mathrm{rms}}}{U_{N,\mathrm{rms}}}\right)
		\end{equation}
		\item Similarly for currents: \;$\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}} = 20\log_{10}(I_{S,\mathrm{rms}}/I_{N,\mathrm{rms}})$.
	\end{itemize}
	\textbf{Note:} Amplifier gain does \emph{not} improve input SNR; it amplifies signal and noise alike, and adds its own noise.
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Example of SNR Calculation %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Example of SNR Calculation}
	Suppose an amplifier input has:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item wanted signal power: $S = 3\unit{mW}=3\times10^{-3}\unit{W}$,
		\item noise power: $N = 30\unit{\mu W}=30\times10^{-6}\unit{W}$.
	\end{itemize}
	\begin{align*}
		\mathrm{SNR} &= \frac{S}{N} = \frac{3\times10^{-3}}{30\times10^{-6}} = 100 \\
		\Rightarrow \mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}} &= 10\log_{10}(100)=10\times 2 = 20\unit{dB}
	\end{align*}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $20\unit{dB}$ may be acceptable for voice links, but high-quality audio typically needs $>60\unit{dB}$.
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Overview of External Noise Sources %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Overview of External Noise Sources}
	\begin{itemize}
	\item External noise includes all unwanted signals \textbf{picked up} from the environment.
	\item Coupling paths:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Capacitive coupling} via stray capacitances $C_s$.
		\item \textbf{Inductive coupling} via changing magnetic fields and loop areas.
		\item \textbf{Radiated EMI} through space at RF.
		\item \textbf{Conducted interference} through shared supplies/grounds.
	\end{itemize}
	\item We focus on three representative groups:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item mains interference (50/60\unit{Hz}),
		\item other man-made interference,
		\item natural noise sources.
	\end{enumerate}
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Mains Interference (50/60 Hz Pick-up) %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Mains Interference (50/60 Hz Pick-up)}
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item A common external noise is coupling from the mains power line.
				\item Stray capacitances $C_s$ between power lines and signal wiring inject a small 50\unit{Hz} component.
				\item Not only the fundamental (50\unit{Hz}) but also harmonics (100, 150\unit{Hz}, \ldots) may appear.
			\end{itemize}
			\textbf{Mitigation preview:}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item increase physical separation of power and signal wiring,
				\item reduce loop area,
				\item use shielding and single-point grounding (next section).
			\end{itemize}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{figure}
			\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{fig/lec03/noise2.pdf}
			\caption{Capacitive coupling of mains interference (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
			\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Conductive Sheath / Shield Against Mains Pick-up %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Conductive Sheath / Shield Against Mains Pick-up}
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.45\textwidth}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Enclosing sensitive wiring/equipment in a conductive sheath tied to a \textbf{single earth point} can block capacitive coupling.
				\item The sheath provides a low-impedance return for displacement currents induced via $C_s$.
			\end{itemize}
			\begin{block}{Key takeaway}
				Good shielding requires a clear reference node; multiple earth points may create earth-loop noise.
			\end{block}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{figure}
			\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{fig/lec03/noise3.pdf}
			\caption{Shielded cable to reduce mains pick-up (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
			\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Other Man-made Noise Sources (1) %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Other Man-made Noise Sources (1)}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Generated by nearby electrical/electronic equipment.
		\item Examples:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item sparking commutators and brushes in DC/universal motors,
			\item fluorescent lamps and switching power supplies,
			\item switchgear producing rapid $\mathrm{d}i/\mathrm{d}t$ and $\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}t$ transients,
			\item radio/telemetry equipment.
		\end{itemize}
		\item Often broadband and impulsive; can fall inside measurement band.
	\end{itemize}
	\vspace{0.2cm}
	\textbf{Three important mechanisms:}
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item adjacent channel interference,
		\item cross-talk between cables,
		\item intermodulation products in non-linear devices.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Other Man-made Noise Sources (2) %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Other Man-made Noise Sources (2)}
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\textbf{Adjacent Channel Interference}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item In radio/data systems, channels operate at carrier frequencies $f_1, f_2, \ldots$.
				\item A strong interferer on a nearby channel may leak into the wanted channel if filtering is insufficient.
				\item Mitigation:
				\begin{itemize}
					\item better frequency-selective front-end filtering,
					\item adequate geographical/physical separation,
					\item proper antenna orientation/polarization.
				\end{itemize}
			\end{itemize}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\textbf{Cross-talk Between Cables}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item When two or more cables run close together, changing currents create magnetic fields that induce voltages in adjacent conductors.
				\item Electrostatic coupling between conductors adds capacitive cross-talk.
				\item Mitigation:
				\begin{itemize}
					\item route sensitive and noisy cables separately,
					\item use twisted pairs and screened (shielded) cables,
					\item minimize parallel run length.
				\end{itemize}
			\end{itemize}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Intermodulation Noise %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Intermodulation Noise}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Two signals at $f_1$ and $f_2$ passing through a non-linear device produce new frequencies:
		\begin{equation}
			f_1,\; f_2,\; f_1+f_2,\; |f_1-f_2|,\; 2f_1,\; 2f_2,\; \dots
		\end{equation}
		\item Some products may lie within the measurement band and degrade SNR.
		\item Mitigation:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item avoid device saturation/non-linear regions,
			\item band-limit before non-linear stages,
			\item maintain adequate headroom.
		\end{itemize}
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Natural Noise Sources %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Natural Noise Sources}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Predominantly affects radio-frequency and long-distance sensing.
		\item Two common types:
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item \textbf{Atmospheric / static noise} from lightning and electric storms.
			\item \textbf{Galactic / cosmic noise} from stars and solar activity.
		\end{enumerate}
	\end{itemize}
	\begin{block}{Frequency ranges}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Atmospheric noise is significant up to about $25\unit{MHz}$.
			\item Cosmic noise dominates beyond $\sim 1\unit{GHz}$.
		\end{itemize}
	\end{block}
\end{frame}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Suppression strategies: shielding %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Suppression Strategies: Shielding}
	\small
	External noise suppression depends on the frequency content and power of the interference and on the sensitivity/bandwidth of the measurement chain. Three practical levers: \textbf{shielding}, \textbf{good circuit layout}, \textbf{eliminating earth loops}.
	\begin{columns}[T]
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item \textbf{Electrostatic (capacitive) shielding}: conductive enclosure tied to earth; low-impedance path for displacement currents.
			\item \textbf{Magnetic shielding at low frequency}: high-permeability materials (e.g., mu-metal, a nickel--iron soft alloy) offer a low-reluctance path that diverts the interfering flux.
			\item \textbf{RF shielding (skin effect)}: low-resistance metal shield (Al, Cu, brass); induced eddy currents oppose the incident field.
		\end{itemize}
		\column{0.45\textwidth}
		\textbf{Practical shielding techniques}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Braided shields or coaxial cables for signal lines; mesh shields reduce electrostatic noise while staying lightweight.
			\item Maintain a \textbf{continuous shield connection} to the reference (avoid floating ends unless designed as such).
		\end{itemize}
		\begin{block}{Pitfall}
			Multiple shield earth points can create circulating currents (earth loops), re-introducing noise.
		\end{block}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Good Circuit Layout %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Good Circuit Layout}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Keep mains / high-current tracks \textbf{far from} low-level signal paths.
		\item Avoid shared routing of power and signal through the same trunking or conduit.
		\item Minimize loop areas in sensitive circuits to reduce inductive pick-up.
		\item Use star-ground or single-point reference where possible.
	\end{itemize}
	\begin{block}{Rule of thumb}
		Treat layout as part of the circuit: parasitic $C_s$ and mutual inductance are layout-controlled components.
	\end{block}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Earth Loops (Ground Loops) Concept and Mitigation %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Earth Loops (Ground Loops) Concept and Mitigation}
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.6\textwidth}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item An earth loop occurs when a system has \textbf{more than one earth bond}.
				\item Different earth potentials ($e_A \neq e_B$) drive a loop current that couples into the signal path.
				\item The loop acts like an aerial, picking up magnetic/electric interference.
			\end{itemize}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Use a \textbf{single-point earth} (star grounding).
				\item Avoid ground connections through multiple equipment chassis.
				\item If unavoidable, break loops with:
				\begin{itemize}
					\item differential inputs / instrumentation amplifiers,
					\item isolation (transformer, opto-isolator),
					\item careful cable shielding strategy.
				\end{itemize}
			\end{itemize}
		\column{0.4\textwidth}
			\begin{figure}
			\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{fig/lec03/noise4.pdf}
			\caption{Example of an earth loop (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
			\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Thermal (Johnson) Noise in Resistors %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Thermal (Johnson) Noise in Resistors}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Caused by random thermal motion of charge carriers.
		\item Produces a noise voltage with zero mean but non-zero RMS.
		\item Spread uniformly over frequency \(\rightarrow\) \textbf{white noise}.
	\end{itemize}
	\begin{block}{RMS noise voltage over bandwidth $B$}
		\begin{equation}
			\boxed{E_n = \sqrt{k_{\mathrm{B}} T B R}}
		\end{equation}
		where
		\begin{itemize}
			\item $k_{\mathrm{B}}$ = Boltzmann constant $\approx 1.38\times10^{-23}\unit{J/K}$,
			\item $T$ = absolute temperature,
			\item $B$ = bandwidth,
			\item $R$ = resistance.
		\end{itemize}
	\end{block}
	\textbf{Implication:} doubling $B$ increases $E_n$ by $\sqrt{2}$ and increases noise \emph{power} by 2.
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Thermal Noise: Key Design Insights %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Thermal Noise: Key Design Insights}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Noise scales with \textbf{temperature}: cooling reduces $E_n$.
		\item Noise scales with \textbf{resistance}: larger $R$ gives larger noise voltage.
		\item Noise scales with \textbf{bandwidth}: keep $B$ only as wide as needed.
		\item Equivalent noise power in $R$:
		\begin{equation}
			P_n = \frac{E_n^2}{R} = 4k_{\mathrm{B}} T B \;\unit{W}
		\end{equation}
		(note independence from $R$ for a matched load).
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Shot Noise in Semiconductors %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Shot Noise in Semiconductors}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Due to discrete nature or random variations in the flow of charge / carriers crossing junctions.
		\item Dominant when DC current passes through a diode/transistor junction.
		\item Also noise power uniform across frequency spectrum -- white over frequency (within typical bands).
	\end{itemize}
	\begin{block}{RMS shot-noise current}
		\begin{equation}
			\boxed{i_n = \sqrt{2 e I B}\;\unit{A}}
		\end{equation}
		where
		\begin{itemize}
			\item $e$ = electronic charge $\approx 1.60\times10^{-19}\unit{C}$,
			\item $I$ = DC junction current \;[\unit{A}],
			\item $B$ = bandwidth \;[\unit{Hz}].
		\end{itemize}
	\end{block}
	\textbf{Takeaway:} higher DC current $I$ increases shot noise proportionally to $\sqrt{I}$.
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Flicker Noise (1/f or Pink Noise) %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Flicker Noise (1/f or Pink Noise)}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Arises from random trapping/recombination in semiconductors.
		\item Magnitude increases as frequency decreases.
		\item Often significant below about $10\unit{Hz}$.
	\end{itemize}
	\begin{block}{Spectral form (qualitative)}
		Noise power spectral density typically follows
		\begin{equation}
			S_{1/f}(f) \propto \frac{1}{f^{\alpha}},\quad \alpha\approx 1.
		\end{equation}
	\end{block}
	\textbf{Practical meaning:} low-frequency measurements (slow sensors, DC offsets) are most affected.
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Partition Noise %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Partition Noise}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Occurs in transistors because emitter current divides randomly between base and collector.
		\item Can be viewed as an additional internal current-noise source.
		\item Usually smaller than thermal/shot noise but relevant in precision low-level circuits.
	\end{itemize}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Additional noise-reduction and signal-conditioning methods exist:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item capacitance multiplication, arithmetic/time averaging,
			\item chopper stabilization, modulation/demodulation,
			\item digital filtering and oversampling.
		\end{itemize}
		\item We will explore these in advanced sessions or projects.
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Passive Filters: Purpose and Types %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Passive Filters: Purpose and Types}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item A \textbf{filter} is a network that passes AC signals only over a defined frequency range and attenuates others.
		\item Widely used in:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item telecommunications and audio,
			\item instrumentation and sensor interfaces,
			\item power electronics and EMI/noise suppression.
		\end{itemize}
		\item Two broad classes:
		\begin{description}
			\item[Passive filters:] only \textbf{R}, \textbf{L}, \textbf{C} elements, no active gain devices.
			\item[Active filters:] use op-amps/transistors in addition to RLC.
		\end{description}
		\item Four basic responses (for both passive and active filters):
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item Low–pass (LPF),
			\item High–pass (HPF),
			\item Band–pass (BPF),
			\item Band–stop / notch (BSF).
		\end{enumerate}
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% First-order passive low-pass CR filter %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{First--Order Passive Low--Pass CR Filter}
	\small
	\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
		\column{0.52\textwidth}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Circuit: series resistor $R$ followed by shunt capacitor $C$; input $u_i$ across ($R$--$C$), output $u_o$ across $C$.
		\end{itemize}
		Capacitive reactance and complex voltage division:
		\begin{align}
			X_C &= \frac{1}{2\pi f C} \quad [\si{\ohm}]\\
			u_o &= -j\,\frac{X_C}{R - jX_C}\,u_i
		\end{align}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Low frequency ($f\to 0$): $X_C\to\infty$, so $u_o \approx u_i$ (pass).
			\item High frequency ($f\to\infty$): $X_C\to 0$, so $u_o \approx 0$ (blocked).
		\end{itemize}
		\column{0.45\textwidth}
		\begin{figure}
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{fig/lec03/first_CR_filter.pdf}
			\caption{First--order passive low--pass CR filter (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
		\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Low–Pass Magnitude Response and Cut–off Frequency (1) %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Low–Pass Magnitude Response and Cut–off Frequency (1)}
	From the complex relation:
	\begin{equation}
		\left|\frac{u_o}{u_i}\right|
		= \frac{X_C}{\sqrt{R^2 + X_C^2}}
		= \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (2\pi f R C)^2}}
	\end{equation}
	\begin{block}{Cut–off (corner) frequency}
		Defined at the \(-3\si{\decibel}\) point:
		\begin{equation}
			\left|\frac{u_o}{u_i}\right| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707
		\end{equation}
		which occurs at
		\begin{equation}
			f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi R C} \quad [\si{\hertz}]
		\end{equation}
	\end{block}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item For $f \ll f_c$: $\left|\frac{u_o}{u_i}\right|\approx 1$ (flat pass–band).
		\item For $f \gg f_c$: gain falls with slope \(\approx -20\si{\decibel}/\text{decade}\).
		\item Phase shifts from \(0^\circ\) (low $f$) to \(-90^\circ\) (high $f$), with \(-45^\circ\) at $f_c$.
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

	\end{figure}
\end{frame}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Second-order passive low-pass CR filter (cascade) %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Second--Order Passive Low--Pass CR Filter (Cascade)}
	\small
	\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
		\column{0.46\textwidth}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item A steeper roll--off is obtained by cascading two first--order sections, e.g., two identical $CR$ low--pass sections.
			\item Each section contributes $-20$\,dB/decade above its cut--off; with equal $f_c$ the asymptotic slope is $-40$\,dB/decade.
			\item In practice the second section loads the first: the $-3$\,dB frequency drops well below $f_c$ ($\approx 0.37 f_c$ for two identical unbuffered sections) and the knee is more rounded than an ideal second--order response.
			\item Cascaded RC sections are simple but give limited control over pass--band flatness (e.g., Butterworth vs.\ Chebyshev characteristics).
		\end{itemize}
		\column{0.52\textwidth}
		\begin{figure}
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/lec03/sec_order_CR_filter.pdf}
			\caption{Cascaded CR low--pass filter and Bode response (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
		\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Second-order low-pass LCR filter (1) %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Second--Order Low--Pass LCR Filter (RLC Network) (1)}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Series inductor $L$ feeding a parallel $C$--$R$ network.
		\item $L$ and $C$ set the natural (resonant) frequency near the desired cut--off:
		\begin{equation}
			f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}} \quad [\si{\hertz}]
		\end{equation}
		\item $R$ (in parallel with $C$) sets the damping ratio $\zeta = \frac{1}{2R}\sqrt{L/C}$:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item High $R$ ($\zeta < 1$): \textbf{underdamped} -- peaking and ringing.
			\item $R = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{L/C}$ ($\zeta = 1$): \textbf{critically damped} -- fast response without overshoot.
			\item Low $R$ ($\zeta > 1$): \textbf{overdamped} -- slow, no overshoot.
		\end{itemize}
		\item Advantages: steeper roll--off ($-40$\,dB/decade) and adjustable ``knee'' region.
		\item Drawbacks: needs an inductor (bulky, lossy, sensitive to magnetic coupling).
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%% First-order passive high-pass CR filter %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{First--Order Passive High--Pass CR Filter}
	\small
	\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
		\column{0.56\textwidth}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Obtained by simply \textbf{interchanging} $R$ and $C$ of the low--pass filter: series capacitor $C$, resistor $R$ to ground, output across $R$.
		\end{itemize}
		With $X_C = 1/(2\pi f C)$:
		\begin{align}
			\left|\frac{u_o}{u_i}\right| &= \frac{R}{\sqrt{R^2 + X_C^2}} = \frac{2\pi f R C}{\sqrt{1 + (2\pi f R C)^2}}\\
			f_c &= \frac{1}{2\pi R C} \quad [\si{\hertz}]
		\end{align}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item $f \ll f_c$: $|u_o/u_i| \approx 0$ (low frequencies blocked); slope $+20$\,dB/decade below $f_c$.
			\item $f \gg f_c$: $|u_o/u_i| \approx 1$ (high frequencies passed).
		\end{itemize}
		\column{0.42\textwidth}
		\begin{figure}
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/lec03/CR_High_Pass.pdf}
			\caption{CR high--pass filter circuit (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
		\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%% Second-order band-pass LCR filter %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Second--Order Band--Pass LCR Filter}
	\small
	\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
		\column{0.54\textwidth}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Series $C$--$L$--$R$ network: at resonance the series $LC$ combination has minimal impedance, so the output across $R$ peaks.
			\item Resonant (centre) frequency $f_0 = \dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$.
			\item Lower and upper $-3$\,dB cut--off frequencies $f_{cl}$ and $f_{cu}$; bandwidth $B = f_{cu} - f_{cl}$.
			\item Quality factor $Q = f_0/B$: higher $Q$ $\Rightarrow$ narrower pass--band and more selective filter.
			\item Used for channel selection, tuned amplifiers, and sensor resonance shaping.
		\end{itemize}
		\column{0.44\textwidth}
		\begin{figure}
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/lec03/LCR_Band_Pass.pdf}

		\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%% Second-order band-stop (notch) LCR filter %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Second--Order Band--Stop (Notch) LCR Filter}
	\small
	\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
		\column{0.54\textwidth}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Parallel $L$--$C$ network in series with $R$: at resonance the parallel $LC$ presents a very high impedance, so almost no signal reaches the output.
			\item Resonant (notch) frequency $f_0 = \dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$.
			\item $f \approx f_0$: output strongly attenuated (deep notch).
			\item $f \ll f_0$ or $f \gg f_0$: the $L$ or the $C$ branch conducts, so the signal is transmitted.
			\item Typical use: rejection of a single interfering tone (e.g., \SI{50}{\hertz} mains hum).
		\end{itemize}
		\column{0.44\textwidth}
		\begin{figure}
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/lec03/LCR_Notch.pdf}
			\caption{LCR band--stop (notch) filter and frequency response (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
		\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Passive filters: summary and limitations %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Passive Filters: Summary and Limitations}
	\small
	\begin{block}{Four basic passive filter types}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Low--pass, high--pass, band--pass, band--stop -- built from $R$, $L$, $C$; first-- and second--order sections can be cascaded.
			\item Multi--section networks approximate standard responses (Butterworth, Bessel, Chebyshev, Cauer), trading pass--band flatness, transition sharpness and phase/step response.
		\end{itemize}
	\end{block}
	\begin{block}{Practical limitations}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Inductors are bulky, heavy, lossy and radiate magnetic fields (coupling/EMI issues).
			\item Component tolerances and loading shift the cut--off and detune the filter.
			\item Passive networks provide no power gain -- they cannot amplify the signal.
		\end{itemize}
	\end{block}
	\textbf{Motivation for next topic:} \alert{active filters} with operational amplifiers overcome many of these limitations while implementing the same transfer functions.
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Active Filters: Motivation and Basic Idea %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Active Filters: Motivation and Basic Idea}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Filter goal:} shape the frequency content of an analogue signal
				(noise rejection, anti–aliasing, channel selection, etc.).
		\item \textbf{Passive filters} use only $R$, $L$, $C$:
			\begin{itemize}
				\item No power gain; they always \emph{attenuate}.
				\item Input and output impedances depend on $R$, $L$, $C$ 
						$\Rightarrow$ strong \textbf{loading} effects.
				\item Inductors can be bulky, expensive, and magnetically noisy.
			\end{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Active filters} use $R$, $C$ and an \textbf{active device}
				(transistor or operational amplifier):
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Provide \emph{voltage or current gain} while filtering.
				\item High input impedance, low output impedance
						$\Rightarrow$ minimal loading of signal source and load.
				\item Inductor–like behaviour can be synthesised using $R$ and $C$
						(\emph{gyrator} (impedance converter) concept).
			\end{itemize}
		\item In practice, modern active filters are almost always op‐amp based;
				early examples used transistor emitter followers for buffering.
	\end{itemize}
	% \vspace{0.2cm}
	% \fbox{\parbox[c][2.3cm][c]{0.9\linewidth}{\centering
	% Insert generic \emph{active filter block diagram}: source $\rightarrow$ active network $\rightarrow$ load}}
\end{frame}

%% First-order active low-pass filter %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{First-Order Active Low-Pass Filter (Op-Amp)}
	\small
	\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
		\column{0.54\textwidth}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item A single $CR$ network in the feedback path of an inverting amplifier: input resistor $R$ from $u_i$ to the inverting node, feedback $R \parallel C$, non-inverting input at 0\,V.
		\end{itemize}
		Closed-loop transfer function:
		\begin{equation}
			\frac{u_o}{u_i}(jf) = -\frac{1}{1 + j\,f/f_c}, \qquad f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi C R}
		\end{equation}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item $f \ll f_c$: $|u_o/u_i| \approx 1$ (0\,dB); $f = f_c$: $1/\sqrt{2}$ ($-3$\,dB); $f \gg f_c$: $\propto 1/f$ ($-20$\,dB/decade).
			\item Phase (in addition to the inversion): $-\tan^{-1}(f/f_c)$, i.e., $0^\circ$ at low $f$, $-45^\circ$ at $f_c$, $-90^\circ$ at high $f$.
		\end{itemize}
		\column{0.44\textwidth}
		\begin{figure}
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{fig/lec03/inv_filter.pdf}
			\caption{First--order active low--pass filter using op-amp (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
		\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% First-order active high-pass filter %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{First-Order Active High-Pass Filter (Op-Amp)}
	\small
	\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
		\column{0.54\textwidth}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Swap $R$ and $C$ in the input branch: capacitor $C$ in series with the input, resistor $R$ to the virtual earth; resistive feedback $R$.
		\end{itemize}
		Idealised transfer function:
		\begin{equation}
			\frac{u_o}{u_i}(jf) = -\frac{j\,f/f_c}{1 + j\,f/f_c}, \qquad f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi C R}
		\end{equation}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item $f \ll f_c$: $|u_o/u_i| \approx f/f_c$ (stop--band); $f = f_c$: $-3$\,dB; $f \gg f_c$: $\approx 1$ (0\,dB).
			\item Phase (in addition to the inversion): $+\tan^{-1}(f_c/f)$, i.e., $+90^\circ$ at very low $f$, $+45^\circ$ at $f_c$, $0^\circ$ at high $f$.
			\item The input capacitor blocks DC and slow drifts -- useful for removing offsets.
		\end{itemize}
		\column{0.44\textwidth}
		\begin{figure}
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{fig/lec03/op_amp_high_pass.pdf}
			\caption{First--order active high--pass filter using op-amp (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
		\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%% Second-order active low- and high-pass filters %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Second-Order Active Low- and High-Pass Filters}
	\footnotesize
	\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
		\column{0.54\textwidth}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Adding a second $CR$ branch with appropriate feedback gives a \textbf{second--order} response (low--pass: two frequency--selective elements; high--pass: dual structure).
		\end{itemize}
		Normalised low--pass transfer function (Butterworth):
		\begin{align}
			\frac{u_o}{u_i}(jf) &= \frac{1}{1 - (f/f_c)^2 + \sqrt{2}\,j\,(f/f_c)}\\
			\left|\frac{u_o}{u_i}\right| &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (f/f_c)^4}}
		\end{align}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Flat pass--band without ripple; stop--band slope $-40$\,dB/decade.
			\item Knee sharpness and damping are set by the capacitor and resistor ratios.
			\item The high--pass version has $(jf/f_c)^2$ in the numerator: pass-- and stop--band are interchanged.
		\end{itemize}
		\column{0.44\textwidth}
		\begin{figure}
			\centering
			\includegraphics[height=0.56\textheight]{fig/lec03/sec_order_high_pass.pdf}
			\caption{Second--order active low-- and high--pass filters using op-amps (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
		\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%% Normalisation and Scaling of Active Filters %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Normalisation and Scaling of Active Filters}
	\begin{itemize}
	\item Start from a \textbf{single–pole} active low–pass filter:
		\begin{equation}
		f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi C R} \quad \text{or} \quad
		\omega_c = \frac{1}{C R} \quad [\text{rad/s}].
		\end{equation}
	\item \textbf{Normalised design:}
		\begin{itemize}
		\item Choose $R = 1~\Omega$, $C = 1~\text{F}$ 
				$\Rightarrow \omega_c = 1~\text{rad/s}$.
		\item Easy to tabulate/filter responses as functions of 
				$\omega/\omega_c$.
		\end{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Impedance scaling} (e.g.\ to $500~\Omega$ level):
		\begin{itemize}
		\item Multiply \emph{all} resistances by scaling factor $k_Z$.
		\item Divide \emph{all} capacitances by $k_Z$ to keep $CR$ constant.
		\item Example: $k_Z=500$ gives $R=500~\Omega$, 
				$C = 1/500~\text{F} = 2\,\text{mF}$, still $\omega_c=1~\text{rad/s}$.
		\end{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Frequency scaling} (change $\omega_c$ without changing impedance level):
		\begin{itemize}
		\item To increase $\omega_c$ by factor $k_f$, reduce the product
				$CR$ by $k_f$.
		\item Often achieved by dividing either $R$ or $C$ by $k_f$
				(or both appropriately).
		\item Example: from $\omega_c=1~\text{rad/s}$ to $50~\text{rad/s}$ at
				$R=500~\Omega$ requires $C = 1/(50\times 500) = 40~\mu\text{F}$.
		\end{itemize}
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Active Filter Design: Summary for Practice %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Active Filter Design: Summary for Practice}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Step 1:} Choose required filter \emph{type} and \emph{order}
			(low–pass, high–pass, band–pass, band–stop; first or second order, etc.).
		\item \textbf{Step 2:} Select an appropriate \emph{response family}
			(Butterworth for flat pass–band, Chebyshev for sharper roll–off, etc.).
		\item \textbf{Step 3:} Start from a \textbf{normalised} prototype
			(e.g.\ $R=1~\Omega$, $C$ values from tables, $\omega_c=1~\text{rad/s}$).
		\item \textbf{Step 4:} Apply \emph{impedance scaling} and \emph{frequency scaling}
			to obtain practical $R$, $C$ and desired cut–off $f_c$.
		\item \textbf{Step 5:} Choose an op–amp:
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Bandwidth $\gg$ required filter bandwidth.
				\item Low input bias current and offset for low–frequency filters.
				\item Adequate output swing and slew rate.
			\end{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Step 6:} Verify with Bode plots (simulation + hand sketch) that:
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Pass–band gain and flatness meet requirements.
				\item Cut–off frequency and slope are correct
						($20$ dB/decade per pole).
				\item Phase behaviour is acceptable for the intended application.
			\end{itemize}
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Analogue and Digital Conversions: Motivation}
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.6\textwidth}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Modern control and instrumentation systems increasingly use \textbf{digital microcomputers} for:
				\begin{itemize}
					\item process control,
					\item data acquisition,
					\item communication and supervision.
				\end{itemize}
				\item However, the \textbf{process itself} and most sensors/actuators remain \textbf{analogue}:
				\begin{itemize}
					\item transducers generate analogue voltages/currents,
					\item actuators (valves, motors, heaters) are driven by analogue signals or PWM.
				\end{itemize}
				\item Therefore we need:
				\begin{itemize}
					\item \textbf{ADC} (Analogue-to-Digital Conversion) to bring sensor information into the digital domain.
					\item \textbf{DAC} (Digital-to-Analogue Conversion) to send control actions back to the analogue process.
				\end{itemize}
			\end{itemize}
		\column{0.4\textwidth}
			\begin{figure}

			\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Typical Analogue--Digital Control Loop %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Typical Analogue--Digital Control Loop}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Transducer sensor} converts physical quantity (force, \dots) to analogue voltage or current.
		\item \textbf{ADC} periodically samples the analogue signal and produces a digital word for the microcomputer.
		\item \textbf{Digital microcomputer}:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item executes control algorithm,
			\item compares measured value with stored setpoint,
			\item computes a new control output.
		\end{itemize}
		\item \textbf{DAC} converts the digital control word to an analogue signal.
		\item \textbf{Analogue controller / actuator} uses this signal to adjust process variables (fuel valve, pump speed, heater power, \dots).
	\end{itemize}
		\begin{block}{Key Design Requirements}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Conversion must be \textbf{fast enough} to follow process dynamics.
			\item Conversion must be \textbf{accurate enough} (resolution, noise, linearity).
			\item ADC and DAC must integrate with signal conditioning (filters, amplifiers, isolation).
		\end{itemize}
	\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Sampling of the Analogue Input}
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.6\textwidth}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Goal of sampling: represent a \textbf{continuous-time analogue waveform} by a sequence of discrete-time values.
				\item In figure:
				\begin{itemize}
					\item (a) original analogue waveform,
					\item (b) regularly spaced sampling instants,
					\item (c) sampled amplitudes forming a pulse train.
				\end{itemize}
				\item The \textbf{height} of each sample pulse equals the instantaneous value of the analogue signal at the sampling instant.
				\item The more samples per period, the better the sampled data follow the original waveform.
			\end{itemize}

			\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Nyquist Sampling Theorem and Practical Rule %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Nyquist Sampling Theorem and Practical Rule}
	\begin{block}{Nyquist Sampling Theorem}
		If the highest frequency present in the analogue signal is $f_{\max}$, then:
		\begin{equation}
			f_s > 2 f_{\max}
		\end{equation}
		is sufficient to reconstruct the signal exactly (in the absence of noise and with ideal filtering).
	\end{block}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $f_s$ = sampling frequency, period $T_s = 1/f_s$.
		\item More than \textbf{two samples per cycle} of the highest frequency component are required.
		\item In practice:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item often choose $f_s \gg 2 f_{\max}$ to simplify anti-alias filtering and to allow for non-idealities,
			\item additional margin is useful when the signal bandwidth is not precisely known or may vary.
		\end{itemize}
		\item If the sampling rate is too low, \textbf{aliasing} occurs.
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Aliasing: when sampling is too slow %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Aliasing: When Sampling is Too Slow}
	\small
	\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
		\column{0.6\textwidth}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Aliasing occurs when $f_s < 2 f_{\max}$: high-frequency components are \textbf{misinterpreted} as lower-frequency signals.
			\item Figure: (a) an 8\,kHz triangular wave sampled at only 10\,kHz appears as a low-frequency alias; (b) two different sinusoids A and B generate the same digitised sample sequence C.
			\item Result: the sampled sequence contains \textbf{false frequency content} and cannot uniquely represent the original waveform.
		\end{itemize}
		\begin{block}{Anti-aliasing}
			Use an \textbf{analogue low-pass filter} before the ADC input to limit the input spectrum to below $f_s/2$, so that no high-frequency components fold into the band of interest.
		\end{block}
		\column{0.38\textwidth}
		\begin{figure}
			\centering
			\includegraphics[height=0.5\textheight]{fig/lec03/anti_alia.pdf}
			\caption{Aliasing of a wave sampled too slowly (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
		\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%% Sample-and-Hold Circuit: Why It Is Needed (1) %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Sample-and-Hold Circuit: Why It Is Needed (1)}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Practical ADCs require a \textbf{constant input voltage} during the conversion time.
		\item A \textbf{sample-and-hold} (S/H) circuit:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item samples the analogue signal during a short \emph{aperture time},
			\item stores it on a capacitor,
			\item holds it essentially constant while the ADC performs conversion.
		\end{itemize}
	\end{itemize}
	\begin{block}{Typical Sample-and-Hold Arrangement}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Buffer amplifier A1 isolates the signal source and charges the hold capacitor $C$ rapidly.
			\item MOSFET sampling switch connects $C$ briefly to the analogue input when a sampling pulse arrives.
			\item During \emph{hold}, A2 buffers the capacitor, presenting a stable voltage to the ADC.
			\item Non-idealities: \textbf{droop} due to leakage currents, finite acquisition time, aperture uncertainty (timing jitter).
		\end{itemize}
	\end{block}
\end{frame}

%% Quantisation in an $N$-bit ADC %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Quantisation in an $N$-bit ADC}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item After sampling, each analogue value must be mapped to one of $2^N$ discrete \textbf{quantisation levels}.
		\item Example:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Analogue range: $0$ to $3.5$ mV.
			\item 3-bit ADC $\Rightarrow 2^3 = 8$ levels, spacing $\Delta V = 0.5$ mV.
			\item Each level assigned a 3-bit binary code (000 to 111).
		\end{itemize}
		\item Any analogue value is replaced by the \textbf{nearest} level: \emph{quantising}.
	\end{itemize}
	\begin{block}{Resolution (Quantum Size)}
		For a full-scale range $U_{\text{FS}}$ and $N$ bits:
		\begin{equation}
			\Delta V = \frac{U_{\text{FS}}}{2^N - 1}
		\end{equation}
		\vspace{-0.75cm}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item $\Delta V$ is both the smallest step in output code and the smallest detectable change (ideal case).
			\item Increasing $N$ reduces $\Delta V$ and thus improves measurement resolution.
		\end{itemize}
	\end{block}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Quantisation in an $N$-bit ADC %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Quantisation Error and Resolution Examples}
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Quantisation error is typically taken as $\pm \frac{1}{2} \Delta V$.
				\item For the 3-bit example (range $0$--$3.5$ mV, $\Delta V = 0.5$ mV):
				\begin{itemize}
					\item quantisation error $\approx \pm 0.25$ mV.
					\item binary code 010 represents all inputs between $0.75$ mV and $1.25$ mV.
				\end{itemize}
				\item Increasing bit length:
				\begin{itemize}
					\item 4 bits: error reduces to about $\pm 0.117$ mV.
					\item 8 or 12 bits: error becomes very small (sub-microvolt range in the example).
				\end{itemize}
			\end{itemize}
		\column{0.45\textwidth}
			\vspace{-0.5cm}
			\begin{figure}
				\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{fig/lec03/4_bit_ADC.pdf}
				\caption{Quantisation levels and "staircase" trasfer characteristics for 4-bit ADC (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
			\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%% Quantisation Noise (1) %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Quantisation Noise (1)}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item The analogue signal is continuous; the quantised version has \textbf{discrete steps}.
		\item Figure illustrates:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item (a) original smooth analogue waveform,
			\item (b) discrete sampled points,
			\item (c) reconstructed waveform showing a jagged, castellated shape.
		\end{itemize}
		\item The difference between the original and quantised/reconstructed waveforms appears as \textbf{quantisation noise}.
		\item It is often modelled as an \emph{approximately white} noise source uniformly distributed between $\pm \Delta V/2$ (for many codes and no overload).
	\end{itemize}
	\begin{block}{Design Trade-off}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item More bits $\Rightarrow$ smaller steps $\Rightarrow$ lower quantisation noise but higher converter complexity, cost, and conversion time.
			\item Choice of $N$ depends on required accuracy, noise floor, and bandwidth.
		\end{itemize}
	\end{block}
\end{frame}

%% Digital-to-Analogue Conversion (DAC): Overview %%

\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Digital-to-Analogue Conversion (DAC): Overview}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item DAC performs the \textbf{reverse} of ADC: converts digital word to analogue output voltage or current.
		\item Widely used for:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item generating analogue control signals from a microcontroller,
			\item reconstructing waveforms from digital data (audio, arbitrary waveform generation),
			\item implementing many ADC architectures internally.
		\end{itemize}
		\item Key performance parameters:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item resolution (number of bits),
			\item settling time (time to reach final value within a specified error after a code change),
			\item linearity and monotonicity,
			\item output range and drive capability.
		\end{itemize}
	\end{itemize}
	\begin{block}{Digital Word}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Input to DAC is a binary number (word).
			\item Each bit contributes a weighted current or voltage.
			\item Analogue output represents the sum of all weighted contributions.
		\end{itemize}
	\end{block}
\end{frame}

%% Weighted resistor DAC: principle %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Types of DAC: Weighted Resistor DAC: Principle}
	\small
	\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
		\column{0.52\textwidth}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item 4-bit DAC with an op-amp summing amplifier.
			\item Each bit controls a switch connecting its resistor branch either to the reference voltage $U_R$ (logic 1) or to ground (logic 0).
			\item Binary-weighted resistors: $R$ (MSB), $2R$, $4R$, $8R$ (LSB).
			\item The branch currents $I_1 \ldots I_4$ sum at the inverting node $U_X$ (virtual earth):
		\end{itemize}
		\begin{equation}
			I_f = I_1 + I_2 + I_3 + I_4, \qquad u_o = -I_f R_f
		\end{equation}
		Feedback resistor $R_f$, often $8R$ for convenient scaling: $|u_o| = D \cdot U_R$ for the input word $D$.
		\column{0.46\textwidth}
		\begin{figure}
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/lec03/weight_res_DAC.pdf}
			\caption{4-bit weighted resistor DAC using op-amp summing amplifier (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
		\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% Worked example: 4-bit weighted resistor DAC %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Worked Example: 4-bit Weighted Resistor DAC}
	\textbf{Given:} resistors $R, 2R, 4R, 8R$; feedback resistor $R_f = 8R$; reference voltage $U_R$; input word $1010$ (MSB to LSB) $\Rightarrow$ bits 1 and 3 are 1, bits 2 and 4 are 0.
	\begin{align}
		I_1 &= \frac{U_R}{R}, \quad I_3 = \frac{U_R}{4R}, \quad I_2 = I_4 = 0\\
		I_f &= I_1 + I_2 + I_3 + I_4 = \frac{U_R}{R} + \frac{U_R}{4R} = \frac{5U_R}{4R}\\
		u_o &= -I_f R_f = -\frac{5U_R}{4R} \cdot 8R = -10\,U_R \qquad (1010_2 = 10)
	\end{align}
	\textbf{Comment:} extending this approach to 8 or 12 bits requires many precise resistor values over a wide range, which is why practical DACs prefer the R--2R ladder implementation.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Types of DAC: R--2R Ladder DAC}
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item The R--2R ladder DAC uses only two resistor values, $R$ and $2R$, arranged in a repeated ladder network.
				\item Digital switches connect each ladder node either to ground (bit 0) or to a reference voltage (bit 1).
				\item The ladder presents a constant input resistance to the reference source, independent of word length.
				\item For a 4-bit ladder with input word 1111, the combined current through the feedback resistor $R_f$ produces the maximum output voltage.
			\end{itemize}

%% Types of ADC: Flash (Parallel) ADC %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Types of ADC: Flash (Parallel) ADC}
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item A flash ADC uses a bank of comparators in parallel, each set to a different reference threshold.
				\item The analogue input is compared simultaneously with all thresholds; the pattern of comparator outputs is encoded into a binary word.
				\item Provides extremely fast conversion (sub-20\,ns) at the cost of large hardware: $2^{N}-1$ comparators for $N$ bits.
				\item Common for very high-speed, low-to-medium resolution applications (oscilloscopes, RF receivers).
			\end{itemize}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{figure}
				\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{fig/lec03/flash_ADC.pdf}
				\caption{Flash ADC block diagram and example with 2-bit output (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
			\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%% Types of ADC: Successive Approximation ADC (1) %%

\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Types of ADC: Successive Approximation ADC (1)}
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Uses a binary search to converge on the input voltage.
				\item A DAC generates trial voltages from a programmable register; these are compared with the input using a single comparator.
				\item Starting from MSB, each trial bit is set or cleared depending on whether the DAC output is too high or too low.
				\item Requires exactly $N$ comparison steps for an $N$-bit result; offers good speed and resolution with moderate hardware.
			\end{itemize}

%% Types of ADC: Counter-Ramp (Integrating) ADC %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Types of ADC: Counter-Ramp (Integrating) ADC}
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item A DAC generates a staircase or ramp voltage $U_2$ controlled by a binary counter.
				\item The ramp is compared with the analogue input $U_1$; while $U_2 < U_1$ the counter is allowed to increment.
				\item When $U_2$ exceeds $U_1$, the comparator disables the clock and the counter value is latched as the digital result.
				\item Conversion time is proportional to input level and limited by the maximum count; simpler but usually slower than SAR or flash ADCs.
			\end{itemize}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{figure}
				\includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{fig/lec03/counter_ramp.pdf}
				\caption{Counter-ramp ADC block diagram (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
			\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%% Other Integrating ADC Types %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Other Integrating ADC Types}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Single-slope ramp ADC}: uses a linear ramp generated by an integrator. The time taken for the ramp to reach the input voltage is measured with a counter. Simple but sensitive to ramp slope and clock errors.
		\item \textbf{Dual-slope (dual-ramp) ADC}: first integrates the unknown input for a fixed time, then integrates a reference voltage of opposite polarity until the integrator output returns to zero. The second integration time gives the digital result.
		\item Dual-slope conversion averages the input over many clock cycles and strongly rejects noise and component drift, at the expense of slower conversion.
		\item These concepts motivate more advanced integrating schemes such as \textbf{delta–sigma} converters.
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%% Types of ADC: Delta--Sigma Charge-Balance ADC: Principle %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Types of ADC: Delta--Sigma Charge-Balance ADC: Principle}
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Aim: obtain high resolution by converting the \emph{average} analogue input over many clock cycles into a digital count.
				\item The analogue input current charges the integrator capacitor; the integrator output is compared with zero.
				\item Depending on comparator output, fixed-size current pulses (derived from a reference source) are switched into or out of the summing junction.
				\item The feedback pulses balance the input on average, keeping the integrator output bounded around zero.
			\end{itemize}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{figure}
				\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{fig/lec03/del_sig_ADC.pdf}
				\caption{Delta--sigma charge-balance ADC block diagram (adapted from \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/C2009-0-25156-7}{Newby, B.W.G.: Electronic Signal Conditioning, 1st ed., Butterworth-Heinemann, 1994})}
			\end{figure}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%% Types of ADC: Delta--Sigma Charge-Balance ADC: Principle %%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Types of ADC: Delta--Sigma Charge-Balance ADC: Operation}
	\begin{columns}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Over a window of $N$ clock periods, a total of $n$ charge pulses is applied to balance the input.
				\item The condition of zero net charge at the summing node implies that $n$ is proportional to the average analogue input current.
				\item The digital output is simply the pulse count $n$ (or its binary representation), giving very fine effective resolution.
				\item Key advantages: excellent noise averaging, high linearity and suitability for low-frequency, high-accuracy measurement (e.g.\ digital multimeters).
			\end{itemize}
		\column{0.5\textwidth}
			\begin{figure}
				\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{fig/lec03/del_sig_ADC.pdf}

\end{frame}

%% Section summary %%

\begin{frame}
	\frametitle{Section Summary}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item A measurement chain converts a physical quantity via sensor, \textbf{signal conditioning}, conversion and processing into information for display or control.
		\item \textbf{Bridges} turn small resistance/impedance changes into voltages; AC bridges (Maxwell, Hay, Schering, Wien) measure $L$, $C$, losses and frequency.
		\item \textbf{Operational amplifiers} (ideal: $U_+ = U_-$, no input current) realise scaling, summing, conversion, integration and differentiation; the gain--bandwidth product limits the closed-loop bandwidth.
		\item \textbf{Noise}: external noise is reduced by shielding, layout and avoiding earth loops; internal noise (thermal, shot, flicker) sets the detection limit.
		\item \textbf{Filters}: passive RC/LCR filters only attenuate; active filters add gain, buffering and well-defined responses (e.g., Butterworth).
		\item \textbf{A/D and D/A conversion}: sample at $f_s > 2 f_{\max}$ (in practice $f_s \gg 2 f_{\max}$) behind an anti-aliasing filter; $N$-bit quantisation sets resolution and quantisation noise; flash, SAR, integrating and delta--sigma converters trade speed, resolution and cost.
	\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
